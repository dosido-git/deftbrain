How to Split Household Chores Fairly

To split household chores fairly, list every chore including the invisible ones, estimate the time each takes per week, let each person pick what they prefer, then balance total time rather than number of tasks. Put recurring chores on a shared calendar so no one person has to notice everything.

  1. List every chore, including small invisible ones.
  2. Estimate minutes per week for each.
  3. Put recurring tasks on a shared schedule.
  4. Let people pick preferred chores first.
  5. Balance total time, not task count.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

A fair chore split balances the total time and effort each person contributes, not the number of tasks. Listing every chore, including easily overlooked ones such as restocking supplies and taking out bins, and estimating minutes per week makes the load visible. Housework is often uneven: the US Bureau of Labor Statistics' American Time Use Survey finds women more likely than men to do housework on an average day. A written agreement helps a split last.

When you moved in, you took the kitchen and your roommate took the bathroom. It seemed even. Six months later you're doing more, and you can't quite say why, because on paper the split was fair. Usually it's the chores that never made the list.

How to do it
1

What chores should you include when splitting housework?

Sit down together with paper. List every chore that happens in the house, in detail. Not just "kitchen" — washing dishes, wiping the counters, taking out kitchen trash, refilling dish soap, mopping the floor, descaling the kettle. Include the once-a-month chores: replacing the air filter, changing batteries in the smoke detector, scheduling pest control. The list is usually three times longer than people expect, and the long version is the actual workload.

2

How do you measure chores fairly?

Some chores are 5 minutes; some are 45. A weekly bathroom clean is different from wiping the counter. Put a time estimate next to each chore — minutes per week, averaged. This is the number that matters for splitting fairly. Two chores that occur the same number of times can carry totally different real loads. The goal is even time, not even chore count.

3

What is the invisible work in a household?

Some chores have an invisible cognitive layer: noticing they need to happen. The person who notices is doing labor before any chore gets done. If one person does all the noticing, they are working harder than the chore split shows. The fix is making the calendar do the noticing — schedule the chore, tie it to a recurring date, put it on a shared list. The household stops depending on one person's vigilance.

4

Does a fair chore split have to be 50/50?

A fair split is not necessarily a 50/50 chore-by-chore split. Some people genuinely do not mind doing dishes; some hate it. Some are good at organizing; some are good at deep-cleaning. Let people pick the chores they prefer first, then split the rest. Total time should be roughly equal across the household. The rule is fairness in load, not symmetry in tasks. A roommate who does all the cooking and none of the cleaning may be working fairly.

5

How do you keep a chore split from falling apart?

Write it down. A shared note, a list on the fridge or a recurring calendar works better than a remembered conversation, which tends to drift within a couple of weeks. Include each chore, who does it, and how often. Agree on a check-in after a month to adjust anything that isn't working.
