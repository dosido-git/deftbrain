How to Break a Lease Legally (Without Wrecking Your Rental History)

To break a lease legally, first check whether a law lets you end it early, such as the federal Servicemembers Civil Relief Act or state protections for domestic violence survivors or uninhabitable units. Otherwise, read your early-termination clause, offer a replacement tenant, and negotiate a written release with your landlord.

  1. Check for a legal right to end the lease early.
  2. Read your early-termination or buyout clause.
  3. Offer a qualified replacement tenant.
  4. Document any landlord breach in writing.
  5. Get any release agreement in writing.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Some tenants have a legal right to end a lease early. The federal Servicemembers Civil Relief Act lets service members terminate a residential lease after entering active duty or receiving qualifying orders, by giving written notice and a copy of the orders. Many states provide similar rights for domestic violence survivors or when a unit becomes uninhabitable. Otherwise, the lease's early-termination clause, a replacement tenant or a negotiated release are the usual routes. Rules vary widely by state.

Something changed: a job in another city, a relationship, a unit with problems nobody mentioned. The lease still has six months on it, and the termination clause says you owe two months' rent. Before you write the check or sublet, check which kind of exit is open to you.

How to do it
1

When can you legally break a lease without penalty?

Wanting to leave and being entitled to leave are different. Common legal grounds include military service under the federal Servicemembers Civil Relief Act, being a survivor of domestic violence (in many states), a unit that has become uninhabitable, or a serious landlord breach. Some states add others. If one applies, you may be able to end the lease without penalty by following a specific notice process. Check your state's tenant guide or a local legal aid office before negotiating.

2

How does an early-termination clause work?

Many leases include a buyout clause: you can leave by paying a set amount, often a month or two of rent. That number may be negotiable. If there's no such clause, what you owe depends on state law; in many states, a landlord must make reasonable efforts to re-rent the unit and can recover only actual losses rather than all remaining rent. Know what your lease and state law say before assuming the worst-case figure.

3

Can you find someone to take over your lease?

Often, with the landlord's approval. Landlords mostly want to avoid an empty unit. If you bring a qualified replacement, the conversation shifts from 'tenant wants out' to 'tenant is helping me avoid a vacancy.' Check whether your lease allows assignment or subletting and what approval it requires, and get any release from the original lease in writing.

4

What if your landlord won't fix serious problems?

If the unit has serious habitability problems — no heat, persistent leaks, mold, broken locks, infestations — and the landlord hasn't fixed them despite written notice, you may have grounds for what's called constructive eviction. The key word is *written*. Email, certified letter, anything dated and saved. A verbal complaint that the heat was out for three days is hard to prove. A written complaint sent six weeks ago that the landlord ignored is leverage. Build the paper trail before you need it.

5

What if your landlord has already broken the lease?

Sometimes the most important fact about your lease is that the landlord has already breached it. They never returned a previous deposit. They've been entering without notice. They've ignored required repairs. They never gave you a copy of the signed lease at all. In some of these cases, you may not be 'breaking' anything — you may be exiting a contract the other party already voided. Talk to a tenant rights organization in your jurisdiction before you assume the lease is the binding document. It might already be the landlord's problem, not yours.
