How to Write a Move-In Condition Report (That Actually Holds Up Later)

To write a move-in condition report that holds up, give each flaw a location and approximate size, link each note to a numbered photo, write see notes instead of ticking good condition for rooms with flaws, then sign, date, copy and email it, asking for confirmation.

  1. Give location and size for every flaw.
  2. Reference a numbered photo for each note.
  3. Don't tick 'good condition' for rooms with flaws.
  4. Sign, date, copy and email it; ask for confirmation.
  5. If the landlord won't acknowledge it, send it by certified mail.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

A move-in condition report is a written record of a rental's condition at the start of a tenancy, used later to separate existing damage from damage during your stay. Specific descriptions tied to dated photos make it far more useful in a deposit dispute. Some states require one: Washington landlords must give tenants a written checklist describing the unit's condition before collecting a deposit. Deposit deadlines and allowed deductions vary by state.

The lease came with a one- or two-page form: checkboxes for each room and a few blank lines. The instinct is to tick 'good,' jot 'small scuff in living room,' and sign. A year later, that casual form may be the main evidence about what was already there. A little more detail now goes a long way.

How to do it
1

How detailed should a move-in condition report be?

Vague descriptions are the most common reason condition reports fail. 'Stain on carpet' is unverifiable a year later — the landlord can claim a different stain, or argue the original one was much smaller. 'Approximately 6-inch dark stain on bedroom carpet, 2 feet from the south wall and 1 foot from the closet doorway' is much harder to argue with. Use measurements (inches, feet), use cardinal directions (north wall, east-facing window), and use specific reference points (next to the bathroom door, beside the third kitchen cabinet from the left). The level of detail feels obsessive in the moment and looks like careful documentation later — which is exactly the goal.

2

Should you add photos to a move-in condition report?

Pair the written report with the photo documentation by referencing photo numbers in the report. 'Scuff on east wall of living room (see photo 14)' creates a direct link between the description and the visual proof. This matters because the report and photos are typically reviewed at different times by different people, and the link between them needs to be explicit. If the photos and the report don't reference each other, the landlord can dispute either one without addressing the other. Numbered references force them to be evaluated together — which is what you want, because the combination is much stronger than either alone.

3

Should you mark rooms as 'good condition' on a move-in report?

Not if anything in them is imperfect. A ticked 'good condition' box can later be read as your statement that the room had no issues. For any room with even minor flaws, write 'see notes' (or add it if the form has no such option) and list the specifics. The form invites summary; specifics protect you better.

4

How do you submit a move-in condition report?

A signed report on paper, with date, is the standard format — but the paper trail matters as much as the document itself. Make a copy for yourself before handing it back. Email a digital copy (with the photos attached) to the landlord, with a subject line like 'Move-in condition report - [unit address] - [date].' Request written confirmation of receipt. If the landlord has a property management portal, upload it there too. The point is redundancy: the paper original might get lost, the email might be claimed never received, the portal might be inaccessible later — but it's nearly impossible for all three to disappear. Three places, one document, one sentence requesting confirmation.

5

What if your landlord won't accept your condition report?

Occasionally a landlord resists a detailed report, calls it 'too detailed,' or won't acknowledge it. Keep your documentation anyway. Send the report, with photos, by email and by certified mail with a return receipt, so you have a record of delivery. A landlord's refusal to acknowledge it doesn't erase your dated record, though how much weight it carries in a dispute depends on the facts and your state's rules. If the landlord resists careful documentation, that's all the more reason to keep it.
