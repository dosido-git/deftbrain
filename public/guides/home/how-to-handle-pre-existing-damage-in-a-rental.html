How to Handle Pre-Existing Damage in a Rental (Without Inheriting Someone Else's Charges)

To handle pre-existing damage in a rental, photograph and describe it on move-in day, note it on the condition report, ask the landlord in writing to repair anything broken, and leave it unpatched. If charged for it at move-out, send your dated records and cite your state deposit law.

  1. Photograph and describe it on move-in day.
  2. Label it as wear or damage accurately.
  3. Ask in writing for repairs to broken items.
  4. Don't fix documented damage yourself.
  5. If charged later, send your records and cite state law.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Pre-existing damage is any damage present when you move in. Landlords can generally deduct only for damage you caused beyond normal wear and tear, so a dated record of what was already there is your main protection. State rules differ: California requires an itemized statement of deductions within 21 days of move-out, and Washington requires a written condition checklist at the start of a tenancy before a landlord can keep a deposit for damage. Check your state's rules.

On move-in day you notice damage that clearly isn't new: a chip in the tub, a hole someone half-patched, an old carpet stain. It isn't yours, and you have a nagging sense that if you don't act now it could become yours by default at move-out. The fix is to remove the ambiguity, early and in writing.

How to do it
1

How do you document damage that was already in a rental?

Pre-existing damage gets the same documentation treatment as everything else: timestamped photos with wide and close-up shots, written description with measurements and locations, and explicit notation on the move-in condition report. Don't downgrade the documentation because the damage is small or because you 'noticed it on the way out.' The cracked tile by the front door deserves the same three photos as anything else. The chip in the bathtub deserves a measurement. Documentation that's rigorous on big damage and casual on small damage is documentation that gets contested at the edges, which is where landlords often try to push deductions.

2

What is the difference between damage and normal wear and tear?

Damage generally means harm beyond ordinary use, such as a hole, a burn or a broken fixture. Normal wear and tear is the gradual deterioration that comes from living somewhere: minor scuffs, faded paint, worn floor finish. Landlords can usually deduct for damage but not for normal wear. Describe pre-existing issues accurately, for example 'paint shows normal wear with several small scuffs' rather than 'walls have many marks.' Precise wording is harder to dispute later.

3

Should you ask your landlord to repair pre-existing damage?

Some pre-existing issues aren't just cosmetic; they're maintenance items the landlord is obligated to fix (broken appliances, leaky fixtures, cracked tiles in the shower, missing hardware, infestations). For these, document them and request repair, in writing, within the first week. 'Hi [Landlord], during move-in I documented several items that need attention: [list with photo references]. Please let me know your timeline for addressing these.' This does two things: it gets the items repaired (or at least creates a record that you asked), and it eliminates any later argument that the issues are your responsibility. Items the landlord agreed to fix but didn't are still landlord-owned issues at move-out; items you never reported are ambiguous.

4

Should you fix pre-existing damage in a rental yourself?

There's a temptation to spackle that hole, paint over the scuff, or patch the carpet stain — partly out of pride in the new place, partly to make it nicer to live in. Don't, at least not for any pre-existing damage you've documented. Two reasons. First, your repair work might not match the original finish, and a landlord can charge you for the difference between your repair and a professional one. Second, once you've 'fixed' something, the question of whether it was pre-existing becomes harder to settle — your repair has obscured the original condition. Live with the imperfections. Document them. Let the landlord be responsible for the unit's condition. If you want to upgrade the apartment for your own enjoyment, do it with reversible improvements that don't touch documented damage areas.

5

What if your landlord charges you for damage that was already there?

Respond calmly and in writing. Send the dated photos, your signed condition report and any emails where the issues were raised. Cite your state's security deposit law, which in most states requires an itemized statement within a set time; some states, such as California, also require receipts or estimates for certain deductions. If the landlord won't correct it, small claims court is the usual next step, and some states allow extra damages for bad-faith withholding. Clear, dated records are often enough to resolve the dispute before it gets that far.
