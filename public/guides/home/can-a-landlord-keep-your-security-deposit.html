Can a Landlord Keep Your Security Deposit (And When You Can Fight It)

A landlord can keep a security deposit only for allowed costs, usually unpaid rent, damage beyond normal wear and tear, and sometimes cleaning, and must usually send an itemized statement by a state deadline, such as 14 days in New York or 21 in California. Unjustified deductions can be challenged.

  1. Deposits cover unpaid rent and damage beyond normal wear and tear.
  2. Most states require an itemized statement by a deadline.
  3. Normal wear, like faded paint or worn carpet, isn't chargeable.
  4. Compare charges with your move-in records.
  5. Send a written demand; small claims court is the next step.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Landlords may deduct from a security deposit for unpaid rent and for damage beyond normal wear and tear, and in some states for cleaning needed to return the unit to its move-in condition. Most states require an itemized statement of deductions within a set time after move-out: 14 days in New York and 21 days in California, according to those states' official guides. In California, a court can also award up to twice the deposit in damages for bad-faith withholding. Rules vary by state.

You moved out and left the place clean. Three weeks later, the statement arrives: painting, carpet cleaning, 'general cleaning.' Your deposit has shrunk by more than half. Before you decide whether to let it go, it helps to know what's allowed, what isn't, and what to do next.

How to do it
1

What can a landlord deduct from a security deposit?

Generally: unpaid rent, damage beyond normal wear and tear, and in some states cleaning needed to return the unit to its move-in level of cleanliness. Repainting after years of normal living, replacing carpet that simply aged, or fixing a previous tenant's damage usually doesn't qualify. Each deduction should connect to something the law in your state allows.

2

Does a landlord have to give you an itemized list of deductions?

In most states, yes, within a deadline that varies: 14 days in New York, 21 in California, and longer in some other states. In some states, a landlord who misses the deadline or skips the itemization can lose the right to keep any of the deposit. If you received only a total, send a written request: 'Please provide an itemized statement of charges with receipts or invoices, as required by [state] law.' California, for example, requires copies of receipts or estimates for certain deductions.

3

Can a landlord charge full price to replace old carpet or paint?

Usually not. Carpets wear, paint fades and appliances age, and normal wear isn't chargeable. When an item is damaged, many courts consider its age and expected life, so a landlord may only be able to charge a portion of the replacement cost. As an illustration, if a ten-year carpet had two years left when it was damaged, a fair charge would be closer to a fifth of replacement than all of it. Ask the age of anything charged at full cost.

4

How do you prove damage was there before you moved in?

If you took photos and a move-in checklist when you moved in, your job is now archeology — pull them up and compare. Stains, scratches, holes, broken fixtures that existed on day one cannot become your responsibility on day 365. If you didn't document, you're working from memory, which is harder but not hopeless: ask the landlord for the previous tenant's move-out documentation or the unit's prior condition reports. Sometimes they don't have one. That's revealing.

5

How do you get your security deposit back from a landlord?

Start with a written demand letter that lists the disputed charges, cites your state's deposit law, and sets a clear deadline. If that doesn't work, small claims court is designed for disputes like this, and limits and procedures vary by state. Some states add penalties for bad-faith withholding; California, for instance, allows damages of up to twice the deposit on top of the deposit itself. For small amounts you may decide it isn't worth the time, but a letter costs little to send.
