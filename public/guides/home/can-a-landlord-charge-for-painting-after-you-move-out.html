Can a Landlord Charge for Painting After You Move Out?

Not for ordinary repainting between tenants. Paint that faded, scuffed lightly or simply aged is normal wear and tear, which deposit laws bar landlords from charging. A landlord can charge a reasonable amount to repair paint damage you caused, such as large marks or colors you weren't allowed to use.

  1. Routine repainting is the landlord's cost.
  2. Fading and light scuffs are ordinary wear.
  3. Damage you caused can be charged at a reasonable cost.
  4. One damaged wall does not justify repainting the unit.
  5. Unapproved paint colors can be charged if the lease forbids them.

Updated November 18, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Paint wears out with time no matter who lives in a unit, and repainting between tenants is a normal cost of renting out a home. California's deposit law bars charges for ordinary wear and tear whether it happened before or during your tenancy, including its cumulative effects over more than one tenancy.

What a landlord can charge for is paint damage beyond ordinary use, at a reasonable cost for the area actually damaged. Rules vary by state and sometimes by city, and leases signed before a change may follow older rules. This is general information, not legal advice.

How to do it
1

Can a landlord charge to repaint after you move out?

Not for routine repainting. Faded walls, light scuffs and paint that has simply aged are normal wear and tear, which the deposit laws of California, Texas, New York, Washington and Massachusetts all exclude from deductions. A landlord who repaints between every tenant is paying a normal cost of renting.

2

What paint damage can a landlord charge for?

Damage beyond ordinary use: crayon or marker on the walls, large stains, gouges, or a color you painted without permission when the lease required approval. Texas defines normal wear and tear to exclude deterioration from negligence, carelessness, accident or abuse, which is where these charges come from. Marks that were there when you moved in aren't yours to pay for.

3

Can a landlord charge you to repaint the whole unit?

Not because of one damaged wall. Washington limits deductions to repairing the damaged portion when the damage doesn't cover the whole item, and California limits charges to a reasonable amount to restore the condition at the start of your tenancy. A damaged bedroom wall supports repainting that wall, not every room.

4

What if you painted the walls yourself?

Check your lease. If it required permission or restoring the original color, a landlord can charge a reasonable cost to paint back over your color. If the landlord approved the color in writing, keep that approval; it's the best defense against a charge later. Either way, the charge should cover the painted area, not the whole unit.

5

How do you dispute a painting charge?

Ask for the itemized statement, the invoice, and if the landlord did the work, the hours and hourly rate, which California and Washington require. Compare the charge with your move-in photos and the length of your tenancy. Then reply in writing with your state's statute from the security deposit laws by state table.

Sources

  • California Civil Code § 1950.5 (California Legislative Information)
  • Texas Property Code, Chapter 92 (Texas Legislature)
  • RCW 59.18.280 (Washington State Legislature)
  • New York General Obligations Law § 7-108 (NY Senate)
  • Massachusetts General Laws c. 186, § 15B (Massachusetts Legislature)
