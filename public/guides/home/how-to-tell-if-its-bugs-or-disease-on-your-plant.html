How to Tell If It's Bugs or Disease on Your Plant (Because the Treatments Are Different)

To tell bugs from disease on a plant, look closely at leaf undersides and stems for insects, webbing or sticky honeydew. Pests leave irregular damage and debris; diseases tend to cause spots with defined edges, halos or rings. Isolate the plant while you work it out, because the treatments differ.

  1. Check leaf undersides, joints and new growth for insects.
  2. Sticky residue and webbing point to pests.
  3. Round spots, rings or yellow halos point to disease.
  4. Watch for a day or two: new damage in new places suggests pests.
  5. Isolate the plant; treat pests before disease if both.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Houseplant pests such as spider mites, mealybugs, scale, aphids and whiteflies are usually visible on close inspection, especially on leaf undersides and new growth, according to university extension services including Iowa State's. Sap-feeding pests often leave sticky honeydew. Leaf diseases instead cause spots: fungal spots are often round with defined edges, while bacterial spots tend to look water-soaked, often with a yellow halo. Because the two are easy to confuse, a plant disease clinic can confirm a diagnosis.

You noticed spots on a leaf, or a sticky film on a stem, or fine webbing in new growth. Something's wrong, but it's small enough that you can't tell what. The advice online splits into two treatment paths, and using the wrong one wastes time and can stress the plant further. A closer look usually settles it.

How to do it
1

How do you check a plant for bugs?

Pull the plant into bright light and examine the undersides of leaves, the joints where leaves meet stems, and the new growth at the top. The most common houseplant pests are visible if you look carefully. Spider mites are tiny but leave fine webbing and stippled (pinprick-pale) leaves. Aphids cluster in soft groups on new growth, usually green or black. Mealybugs look like small white cotton tufts in leaf joints. Scale insects look like small brown or tan bumps on stems and look so much like part of the plant that people often miss them. Whiteflies fly up in a small cloud when you disturb the plant. If you see any of these, you have a pest problem, and you can stop diagnosing.

2

What do plant disease spots look like compared with insect damage?

Disease damage tends to follow patterns. Fungal leaf spots are often round with a defined border, sometimes with rings or tiny dark specks inside. Bacterial spots often look water-soaked or greasy, brown to black, frequently with a yellow halo. Powdery mildew is a white or gray dusting. Insect damage tends to be irregular: chewed edges, pale stippling, sticky honeydew, distorted new growth. Spots with defined shapes suggest disease; messy, scattered damage suggests pests. Fungal and bacterial spots are hard to tell apart without lab testing.

3

How can you tell if plant damage is spreading?

Bugs move; spots usually don't. If you mark a spot on a leaf with a pen and check it the next day, fungal and bacterial spots will be in the same place (often slightly larger). Insect damage often appears in new spots overnight as the bugs move around the plant. Insects also leave evidence — frass (tiny dark specks of poop), shed skins, sticky honeydew that ants might be visiting. Disease leaves discoloration but not debris. Spend two days observing the plant before treating, if you can; the movement (or lack of it) often resolves the diagnostic question by itself.

4

Should you separate a sick plant from your other plants?

Yes. Both pests and diseases can spread, so move the affected plant away from others until you know what you're dealing with. For pests, extension services commonly suggest wiping or rinsing leaves, removing heavily infested parts, and using insecticidal soap or horticultural oil according to the label, repeated over several weeks. For leaf diseases, remove affected leaves, improve air circulation and keep leaves dry when watering; fungicides are sometimes used, following label directions. Using the wrong treatment can stress the plant further.

5

Can a plant have both pests and disease?

Yes. Insects feeding on a plant can create wounds and stress that make infection more likely. If you've had pests for a while and now see leaf spots, you may have both. Many growers deal with the pests first, since they can keep causing new damage, and then manage the disease. Recovery from two problems at once is slower, but it's often possible with patience.
