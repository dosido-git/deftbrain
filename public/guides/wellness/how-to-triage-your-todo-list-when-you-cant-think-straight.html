How to Triage Your To-Do List (When You Can't Think Straight)

When you can't think straight, triage with rules instead of judgment: do time-locked tasks first, then anything blocking someone else, then anything under five minutes. Decide or delete items older than three days, and group what's left by type so you can work through one kind at a time.

  1. Time-locked tasks first, in clock order.
  2. Then anything blocking someone else.
  3. Then anything under five minutes.
  4. Items older than three days: do today or delete.
  5. Group the rest by type and run one batch.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

When stress or exhaustion makes it hard to rank tasks, a fixed set of rules can stand in for judgment. A simple order is: tasks with a fixed time window first, then tasks that are blocking other people, then quick tasks under five minutes. Items that have sat on the list for days get a do-today-or-delete decision, and the remaining larger work is grouped by type. Rules reduce the number of decisions you have to make.

Decisions feel impossible. The list is in front of you and your brain won't engage; you can't even tell anymore which items are big or small. Thinking harder isn't available right now, but following rules is.

How to do it
1

What should you do first when you can't decide what to do?

Time-locked means the task has a fixed window that closes if you miss it: a phone call at 3pm, a form due by midnight, a flight to catch. These don't require ranking — they require remembering. Pull every time-locked item out of the list and put them in calendar order. Don't think about whether they're "important." Time-locked tasks are pre-prioritized by the clock; you're just executing.

2

Why should you do tasks that block other people early?

If someone is waiting on you to start their work, that item has a multiplier — your delay is also their delay. Look through the list for tasks where someone is currently stuck on you. Reply to the email that hasn't been answered, ship the file, send the approval. These are usually small actions with disproportionate downstream impact. Doing them clears blockages while you can't think about anything bigger.

3

Should you do quick tasks first?

Tiny tasks are kept on the list because they're easy to ignore, and they accumulate into a felt mass that makes everything heavier. Rip through them. Don't decide whether each one matters — apply the rule. Under 5 minutes, do it now. The reason is psychological as much as practical: the cleared-list feeling restores some cognitive room for the harder remaining items.

4

What should you do with tasks that have been on your list for days?

Items that have sat on the list for more than a few days are often there because you can't decide what to do with them, not because they're hard. Pick each one up and choose: do it today, or take it off the list (or move it to a someday list you don't look at daily). 'Maybe' isn't an option in triage mode. The indecision has been costing you more than the work would.

5

How do you tackle bigger tasks when your head is foggy?

Whatever's left is bigger work that requires real attention. You can't think your way through it right now — but you can group it. Put all email-shaped tasks together, all call-shaped tasks together, all writing-shaped tasks together. Then pick a context and run that batch. Context-switching is what your low-thinking state can't afford; running a batch lets you ride one mode for an hour without re-deciding.
