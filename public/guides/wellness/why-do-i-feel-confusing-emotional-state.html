Why Do I Feel Two Things at Once?

You can feel two things at once, such as relief and grief or pride and embarrassment, because most situations contain more than one thing to respond to. Mixed feelings are normal. Name both rather than forcing one word, don't decide which is the real one, and let them coexist.

  1. Most situations contain more than one real thing.
  2. Name both feelings; don't force one word.
  3. German has a word for one mix: Wehmut, bittersweet melancholy.
  4. Don't decide which feeling is the 'real' one.
  5. Let them coexist; they don't need resolving.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Mixed emotions, such as feeling sad and relieved, or proud and embarrassed, at the same moment, are a normal response to situations that contain more than one meaningful element. Naming each feeling, rather than forcing a single label, tends to make the experience clearer; a 2007 UCLA study led by Matthew Lieberman found that putting feelings into words reduced activity in the amygdala. Some languages have single words for particular blends, such as German Wehmut, a wistful melancholy.

Something has happened that makes you sad and relieved at the same time, or grateful and resentful. The usual script says figure out which one you really feel. But you feel both, and trying to pick one makes it harder.

How to do it
1

Is it normal to feel two emotions at once?

A grandparent dies after a long illness. The sadness is real; the relief is also real. Both are responses to actual features of the situation — the loss of a loved person and the end of their suffering. There's no contradiction; the situation contains both, and your nervous system is correctly registering both. The pressure to pick one is cultural, not psychological. Mixed feelings are usually a sign you're paying attention.

2

Is there a word for feeling happy and sad at the same time?

German has Wehmut, a wistful, bittersweet melancholy: joy shadowed by awareness that the moment is passing. Other languages have their own words for particular mixtures: saudade in Portuguese, Sehnsucht in German, mono no aware in Japanese. English tends to use phrases like 'bittersweet' or 'mixed feelings' instead, which may be part of why mixed states can feel like confusion rather than a recognizable experience.

3

How do you describe mixed feelings?

When you can't find a single word for what you're feeling, the move isn't to compress the experience into the closest available word. The move is to use two or three words. 'Grateful and irritated.' 'Proud and embarrassed.' 'Sad and relieved.' This is more accurate, more useful, and stops the misleading hunt for a single label. Most adult emotional life is multi-word; the single-word framing is a remnant of how we taught feelings to children.

4

Why do you feel guilty about one of two feelings?

When you're feeling two things at once, you'll often catch yourself privately deciding one of them is the 'real' feeling and the other is something to suppress. The grief is real; the relief is shameful. The love is real; the irritation is petty. This sorting is the cultural script doing its work — and it's almost always wrong. Both feelings are real responses to real features of the situation. The hierarchy you're imposing is the source of the discomfort, not the mixture itself.

5

Do mixed feelings need to be resolved?

Mixed feelings get easier when you stop trying to fix them. The grief and the relief can both be there for the next hour, the next week, the rest of your life — they don't have to merge or resolve into a single coherent emotion. Treat them as parallel responses to the same situation rather than as conflicting forces requiring a winner. Most of what feels like emotional confusion is the futile effort to collapse a multidimensional experience into a one-word answer. The collapse is the problem; the multidimensionality is fine.

Sources

  • UCLA Health: Putting feelings into words produces therapeutic effects in the brain (Lieberman et al., 2007)
