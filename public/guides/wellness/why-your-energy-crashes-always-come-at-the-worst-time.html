Why Your Energy Crashes Always Come at the Worst Time

Energy crashes tend to land on big days because those days, and the days before them, carry the heaviest load: travel, family, late nights, broken routines. Mark predictable peak weeks in advance, add nothing extra to them, and keep a light day on either side.

  1. Crashes follow the load, not bad luck.
  2. Peak-load weeks are predictable.
  3. Don't add extras to peak weeks.
  4. Keep a light buffer day on each side.
  5. Tally the whole week before it starts.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Crashes that seem to hit at the worst time, such as a wedding week, a big presentation or a family visit, usually follow the build-up of load in the days before, not chance. These events bring travel, late nights, extra social contact and disrupted routines all at once. Marking predictable high-load weeks in advance, keeping other commitments out of them, and protecting a light day before and after can reduce how often a big week ends in a crash.

It feels like sabotage: the week of your sister's wedding, the morning of the big presentation, the Saturday you'd been looking forward to. That's when the crash comes, never on a quiet Monday.

How to do it
1

Why do you crash on important days?

You did not crash on the day of the wedding because the wedding was unlucky timing — you crashed because the three days leading up to it were already the heaviest days of the month. Travel, family arriving, errands, late dinners, no usual routines. The wedding itself was the trigger; the load was already there. The crash was always going to land on a high-load day. The calendar just provides the trigger.

2

Can you predict when you'll run out of energy?

The week of any wedding, any conference, any visit from family, any move, any launch, any holiday — those are predictable peak-load weeks regardless of what specifically happens in them. Mark them in advance. Crashes often feel surprising because each event gets treated as a one-off, when the crash week may look a lot like the last one. You can see them coming a month out.

3

What should you avoid scheduling during a busy week?

The week of a wedding is not the week to also catch up with the friend you have not seen in months. The week of a launch is not the week to also start a new workout routine. The instinct is to fit more in because you are already busy and have momentum. The reality is the marginal energy cost is highest exactly when you have the least to spare. Treat peak-load weeks as protected weeks for nothing else.

4

Why should you plan a rest day before and after a big event?

The day before a big event and the day after should be light: few commitments, sleep as a priority, little socializing. Many crashes happen because people try to keep their normal week running alongside the big thing. Buffer days aren't optional extras; they're part of the cost of doing the big thing well.

5

How do you plan a high-pressure week so you don't crash?

Write out the whole week, including the small things: travel, errands, calls, meals with family. Mark each as light, moderate or draining and look at the total. If it's clearly too much, you don't have a scheduling problem, you have too many commitments. Move things, decline things, or accept a lower-energy stretch afterward and plan recovery for the days that follow. Seeing it in advance turns a surprise into something you can prepare for.
