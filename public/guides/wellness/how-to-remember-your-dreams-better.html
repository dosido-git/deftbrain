How to Remember Your Dreams (Better)

To remember dreams better, lie still for a minute when you wake before reaching for anything, decide before sleep that you want to remember, keep paper or a phone within reach, write down fragments rather than a story, and reread your notes soon after waking.

  1. Stay still for a minute after waking.
  2. Set the intention before sleep.
  3. Keep paper or a phone within reach.
  4. Write fragments first, not a story.
  5. Reread your notes within the hour.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Dreams are most vivid during REM sleep, and most are forgotten soon after waking unless you capture them quickly. Sleep organizations such as the Sleep Foundation describe simple habits that improve recall: staying still for a moment on waking, keeping a dream journal by the bed, writing down whatever fragments you remember before doing anything else, and intending to remember before you fall asleep. Recall usually improves with a few weeks of practice.

You wake knowing you had a vivid dream. By the time you've reached for your phone there's one image left, and by the time you've brushed your teeth it's gone. Recall is mostly a matter of habit, and a few small ones make a difference.

How to do it
1

Why do you forget dreams so quickly after waking?

Dream memories are fragile, and shifting your attention to the day, by moving, checking your phone or thinking about plans, tends to push them out. Staying still in the position you woke up in, with your eyes closed, gives fragments a chance to surface. Lie still for about a minute. Don't reach for your phone. Often the dream comes back not as a story but as pieces that connect if you give them a moment.

2

Does telling yourself to remember your dreams work?

It's a commonly recommended technique, and many people find it helps: before you fall asleep, tell yourself once that you want to remember your dreams. The likely reason is attention; if you've decided it matters, you're more likely to notice and hold on to dream content when you wake. Keep it simple. One sentence to yourself is enough.

3

Should you keep a dream journal?

The window for capture is small. If you have to get out of bed, walk somewhere, find a pen — the dream is gone. Have something writeable within arm's reach: a pad on the nightstand, a phone with a notes app open from last night. The capture has to be frictionless. Lower the bar to the absolute minimum and you'll catch dreams you'd otherwise lose.

4

What should you write down when you wake from a dream?

When you wake up, you don't have a story — you have impressions. A feeling. A color. An image. A name. Write those down first, in any order. Don't try to construct a narrative. The story will assemble from the fragments later if you have them. If you try to write a story, you'll lose the fragments while you're searching for connecting tissue. Fragments first, narrative later (or never).

5

When should you reread your dream notes?

Notes scribbled at 3 a.m. or 6 a.m. make the most sense if you reread them while the dream is still partly retrievable. Looking at 'green door / father / can't speak' within an hour often rebuilds the dream around those anchors; the same note three days later may mean nothing. Make a quick review part of your morning, before email, and the practice tends to deepen.
