How to Know If You're Burned Out (Or Just Tired)

Tiredness usually lifts with rest; burnout doesn't. If a real weekend off leaves you restored, you were tired. If you return to the same low within hours, enjoyment has faded, work feels pointless, and it has been building for months, burnout is more likely.

  1. Check whether a real weekend off restores you.
  2. Notice whether you still enjoy things.
  3. Ask whether work feels heavy or pointless.
  4. Look at how long it has been building.
  5. Watch how fast you slide back after time off.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

The clearest difference between tiredness and burnout is how each responds to rest. The World Health Organization defines burnout as a syndrome from chronic, unmanaged workplace stress, marked by exhaustion, mental distance or cynicism about work, and reduced effectiveness. Ordinary tiredness improves after sleep or a weekend off; burnout tends to return quickly once work resumes. Loss of enjoyment can also signal depression, which the NIMH says warrants attention when it lasts two weeks or more.

Some weeks a Saturday on the couch resets you. Other times the rest doesn't take, and Sunday night's dread is the same as last week's. The two feel alike on the surface, and guessing wrong means spending your recovery on the wrong fix.

How to do it
1

Does a weekend off fix burnout?

Take a real weekend — no work, low obligation, your normal recovery activities. Sunday night, check in. If you feel meaningfully better than Friday, you were tired. If you feel about the same, or you feel rested but Monday-anxiety is already cancelling it out, you're somewhere on the burnout curve. The weekend is a litmus test, not a treatment. Use it to diagnose.

2

Is losing enjoyment a sign of burnout?

Tired people still enjoy things; they just have less energy for them. With burnout, and also with depression, the enjoyment itself can fade. Dinner with friends used to feel good and now feels like effort, even though the friends are the same. If things you reliably enjoyed aren't producing the feeling they used to, that points beyond ordinary tiredness. If it lasts two weeks or more, or comes with hopelessness, it's worth talking to a doctor, since depression and burnout overlap and need different kinds of help.

3

What is the difference between work feeling hard and feeling pointless?

Tired feels heavy — the work is hard, but it still matters. Burnout feels pointless — you can do the work, but you can't make yourself care. Heaviness responds to rest; pointlessness doesn't. If you'd happily do the work after a real break, you're tired. If even imagining the post-break version of yourself doesn't bring the meaning back, you're past tired and into something that needs a different kind of intervention.

4

How long does it take to burn out?

Tiredness tends to follow a hard week or a tough month. Burnout usually builds over a longer stretch: three months, six months, a year. If you can name when this started and it was recent, you're more likely tired. If you can't say when it started, or it began long ago and has been drifting downward since, you're looking at a trend rather than a state, and that calls for a different response.

5

How can you test whether you're burned out?

Take two days and genuinely rest. Then notice what happens when you go back. Tiredness tends to return to your usual baseline; burnout often slides back to the same low place within hours of returning. If two days off restored you, the work is hard but manageable and you may need more frequent rest. If you felt better and were back where you started by Monday morning, the balance between work and recovery is the problem, not the amount of rest.
