Why does my writing feel cold even when I'm being friendly?

Your writing feels cold because in person your face and voice supply the warmth, and in writing they don't, so it has to be added on purpose. Use names and personal references, keep a friendly opening line, sign off warmly, and reread sent messages as a stranger would.

  1. In writing, warmth has to be added on purpose.
  2. Use names and a personal reference.
  3. Keep a friendly opening line; it sets the tone.
  4. Sign off warmly.
  5. Reread your last ten emails as if a stranger sent them.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Friendly people often write coldly because the cues that carry warmth in person (facial expression, tone of voice, pauses, gestures) don't travel in text. What remains is the plain words, which can read as brisk or impatient. Warmth in writing comes from small deliberate additions: the recipient's name, a personal reference, a brief friendly opener, a warm sign-off, and rereading your sent messages as if someone else wrote them.

In person, people call you warm. Your emails, though, have been called 'efficient,' more than once. Both are true, and the gap is fixable.

How to do it
1

Why do my messages sound cold?

In person, warmth is the default — your face is doing it whether you think about it or not. In writing, warmth has to be intentionally added, because the channel does not transmit any warmth automatically. Many people who are warm in person and cold in writing have not adjusted to this difference. They are writing the way they would talk if they had vocal warmth doing half the work. The vocal warmth is not there. The compensation has to be in the words themselves.

2

How do you make a message sound warmer?

A message that opens with 'Hi Sarah' lands warmer than one that opens with 'Hi.' A message that says 'hope your trip went well' lands warmer than one that just gets to the point. These small acknowledgments take three seconds to add and signal something the reader cannot get from the rest of the message — that they are a person to you, not a task. Many cold-feeling writers skip this because it feels redundant. It is not redundant. It is the warmth.

3

Are phrases like 'hope you're well' just filler?

Phrases like 'hope this finds you well' and 'just wanted to check in' get a bad reputation as filler. They are not filler. They are tonal anchors. They tell the reader the message is friendly before the substance arrives. Writers who pride themselves on being efficient cut these out and end up sounding like requisitions. Bring some of them back. A few extra words can change the emotional read of a message.

4

Does your email sign-off change how you come across?

How you end a message says almost as much as how you start it. 'Thanks' is fine. 'Thanks!' is warmer. 'Thanks so much, talk soon' is warmer still. The signoff is the last thing the reader sees and disproportionately shapes how they remember the whole exchange. A neutral signoff makes a friendly message feel professional. A warm signoff makes a professional message feel friendly. The difference is two or three words.

5

How can you tell if your emails sound cold?

Open your sent folder and read your last ten emails as if you were the recipient and did not know the sender. What is the impression? Probably more clipped, more transactional, more impatient than you felt when writing them. That gap is what your readers are getting. Once you can see it, you can fix it. Add a few words of warmth to the next ten emails you send and notice whether people respond differently.
