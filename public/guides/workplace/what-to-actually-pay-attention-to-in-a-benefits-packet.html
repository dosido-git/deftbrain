What to actually pay attention to in a benefits packet

In a benefits packet, focus on the yearly total cost of each medical plan (premium plus deductible and expected care), any HSA option, the 401(k) match and vesting schedule, and what happens if you miss the deadline. Supplemental insurance add-ons deserve skepticism unless you have a specific need.

  1. Compare medical plans by total yearly cost, not premium.
  2. Look closely at an HSA if one is offered.
  3. Contribute enough to get the full 401(k) match.
  4. Check the vesting schedule for employer money.
  5. Find the deadline and the default if you do nothing.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

The parts of an open-enrollment benefits packet that matter most are the total yearly cost of each health plan, meaning premiums plus the deductible, copays and coinsurance up to the out-of-pocket maximum; any health savings account; the retirement match and its vesting schedule; and the enrollment deadline and default. Each plan's Summary of Benefits and Coverage lays out costs in a standard format, and the US Department of Labor notes your own 401(k) contributions are always fully vested.

The packet is forty-eight pages: three medical plans, two dental, an HSA, an FSA, life, disability, supplemental options and 401(k) updates. The deadline is in two weeks and you're tempted to check last year's boxes again. A few pages deserve real attention; most don't.

How to do it
1

How do you compare health insurance plans at open enrollment?

It's common to compare medical plans by the monthly premium alone, which misses most of the cost. Compare total expected cost: a year of premiums plus the deductible, copays and coinsurance you expect, with the out-of-pocket maximum as the worst case. A high-deductible plan with a lower premium can be cheaper if you do not use much healthcare, and much more expensive if you do. Estimate your actual usage — doctor visits, prescriptions, planned procedures — and run the math for each plan. The right plan is rarely the lowest premium. Sometimes it is the highest.

2

Is an HSA worth it?

If you are eligible for a health savings account through a high-deductible plan, it is often one of the most underused benefits. Under IRS Publication 969, contributions are tax-deductible (or pre-tax through payroll), growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free; the balance stays with you if you change jobs, and unspent money rolls over from year to year, so for people who can afford to contribute and not spend it, an HSA can double as long-term savings. Some employers add their own contribution. The tax treatment varies in a few states, so check the plan's materials. Many people skip the HSA because the high-deductible plan looks scary on paper; compare the plans on total cost first, with the HSA counted in.

3

How do you know if you're getting the full 401(k) match?

Check the current match formula and your contribution percentage. If you contribute less than the amount needed for the full match, you are leaving part of your pay unclaimed. Match formulas change at some employers from year to year, so don't rely on last year's understanding. Also check the vesting schedule: your own contributions are always 100 percent yours, but employer contributions can vest over time. Under federal rules, a 401(k) can require up to three years for full vesting of the match (cliff vesting) or phase it in over as many as six. If you might leave soon, the vesting date matters.

4

Is supplemental insurance through work worth it?

Supplemental life, accident insurance, hospital indemnity, critical illness coverage — these pay out only in specific situations, and the payouts are often smaller than they sound. Unless you have a specific situation that makes one relevant, such as a gap your health or disability coverage leaves, they are usually lower priority than the core choices. Read what each actually pays and when before adding it.

5

What happens if you miss the open enrollment deadline?

Every benefits packet specifies what happens if you take no action — usually you are auto-enrolled in the default. Sometimes the default is fine. Sometimes the default is bad (you lose FSA dollars, you get auto-enrolled in a plan that does not fit). Find the deadline. Find the default. If the default is not what you want, set a reminder and act. One of the most expensive benefits mistakes is letting the deadline pass and being defaulted into something you would have changed.
