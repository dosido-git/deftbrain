How to Spot What's Actually Changed in a Terms of Service Update

To see what changed in a terms of service update, read the company's summary of changes, then compare the old and new versions with a free text-comparison tool. Focus on data, billing, dispute resolution and content rights, look for an opt-out window, and decide before the effective date.

  1. Read the company's own summary of changes first.
  2. Compare old and new versions with a diff tool.
  3. Focus on data, billing, arbitration and content rights.
  4. Search for 'opt out' and any deadline.
  5. Decide before the effective date.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Most terms of service updates change a small number of provisions, so the efficient approach is to find the differences rather than reread the document. A summary of changes, if provided, is the starting point; a side-by-side comparison of the old and new text (from an archive or the Wayback Machine) shows everything else. The FTC has warned that quietly making data practices more permissive through a retroactive change to terms or a privacy policy may be unfair or deceptive.

The email says new terms take effect June 1 and links to a 31-page document. You'll probably click agree, because you use the service. The real question is whether you'll know what you agreed to.

How to do it
1

Do companies tell you what changed in their terms of service?

Many companies include a brief summary of changes at the top of an updated ToS, or in the email announcing it. Find that summary before you read the document. It's the company's own list of what they changed, and it's usually accurate, even when it's incomplete. Read it first. Then judge whether the document deserves further attention.

2

How do you compare an old and new terms of service?

The Wayback Machine, the company's own archive, or an earlier email often has the previous version. Open both. Use a free diff tool — there are several browser-based ones — and let the software show you exactly what changed. This takes about three minutes and surfaces every word-level edit. The changes that aren't in the company's summary are the ones to read most carefully.

3

Which terms of service changes matter most?

Most meaningful terms changes touch one of four areas: what data they collect or share, how they bill you, how disputes get resolved, and what rights you grant them over your content. Other changes are usually cosmetic — defined terms, formatting, jurisdiction details. The four substantive areas are where the real news is. Search the diff for 'data,' 'fee,' 'arbitration,' and 'license.' That covers most changes worth reading.

4

Can you opt out of new terms of service?

Some ToS updates — particularly to arbitration clauses — include a small window in which you can opt out by sending a written notice. The window and procedure, if they exist, are specified in the document, and they are easy to miss. Search for 'opt out' or 'reject this provision.' If it's there, decide whether to use it before the window closes.

5

What happens if you keep using a service after the terms change?

Continuing to use the product after the effective date typically counts as acceptance. If you have concerns, the time to act on them is before the date in the email — by deleting your account, exporting your data, or sending the opt-out notice. After the date, the new terms apply by default and the leverage is gone. Calendar the date the email gave you. The change-or-don't decision is a real one only while you still have time to make it.
