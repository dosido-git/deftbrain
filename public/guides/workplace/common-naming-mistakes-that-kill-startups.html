Common Naming Mistakes That Kill Startups

The naming mistakes that hurt startups most are a spelling people can't guess from hearing it, wordplay only the founders understand, a name tied to one product, a domain that isn't the obvious one, and a name already buried in search results by someone else.

  1. A name people can't spell after hearing it once.
  2. Clever wordplay that customers don't get.
  3. A name tied to the first product.
  4. A domain that isn't what people would type.
  5. A name already crowded in search results.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Startup naming mistakes fall into a small, repeating set: names that must be spelled out loud, puns that need explaining, names locked to a single product, domains that differ from the name people would type, and names whose search results are already owned by a celebrity, another industry or a competitor. Each one adds friction every time someone tries to find, recommend or remember the company, and each is easier to catch before launch than to fix with a rename later.

The name sounded clever in the room and looked fine on the deck. Six months in, the team is tired of spelling it, the domain is one prefix off, and search results belong to someone else. None of that was a failure of creativity. It was a skipped test.

How to do it
1

Why is a creatively spelled business name a problem?

If your name uses a creative spelling — think Lyft, Fiverr or Tumblr — you have committed yourself to spelling it for every customer for the rest of the company. The well-known names that pulled it off had large marketing budgets to teach the spelling; most new companies do not. The diagnostic is simple: when you say the name to someone unfamiliar, do they spell it correctly without prompting? If not, you have just signed up for a permanent tax. Fix: pick a name with one obvious spelling.

2

Can a startup name be too clever?

Wordplay names — puns, portmanteaus, inside jokes — feel smart at the conference room table and translate poorly to customers, who do not have the context. The customer just sees a weird word. Cleverness in naming is a private joy of the founders that the market never gets in on. The diagnostic: can a stranger get the name without you explaining it? If not, the cleverness is a cost, not an asset.

3

Should you name a startup after your first product?

Naming a startup after the first product is a famous mistake — the company outgrows the product, and the name becomes a strait-jacket. A company named for its photo-sharing feature has a harder time when it expands into something else. The diagnostic: would this name still work if the product changed direction in two years? If the answer is no, the name is too narrow. Pick something that allows room to evolve.

4

Is it a mistake to use a domain with get, try or hq in front?

If the .com is taken and you settle for adding get, try, my, or hq to the front, every customer who tries to find you will fail at least once. They will land on the competitor or the parked page. The diagnostic: is the domain you can actually buy the obvious version someone would type? If it is gettrybrand.io, you have a friction tax that will quietly cost you for years. Fix: change the name until the obvious domain is clean. Many founders treat a name whose obvious domain is unavailable as a name they cannot use.

5

How do you check if a name is already buried in search results?

Search the name in your customer's likely search context. If page one is a famous person, a different industry, or a controversial product, you will not rank for your own name for years. It is easy to do this search casually and dismiss the results. The honest test is: how many of your customers will get to your site easily by searching the name? If the answer is low, the name is too crowded. Pick something with a quieter search landscape.
