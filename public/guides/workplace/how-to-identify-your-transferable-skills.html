How to identify your transferable skills

To identify your transferable skills, describe your recent work in plain language without jargon, group it into recurring abilities (aligning people, explaining complex ideas, managing a process), find the terms the target field uses for them, back each with a specific story, and ask someone in that field what lands.

  1. Rewrite your work in plain language, no jargon.
  2. Group it into five to seven recurring abilities.
  3. Borrow the target field's words for those abilities.
  4. Back each skill with a specific story.
  5. Ask someone in the field to react to your list.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Transferable skills are abilities that are useful across jobs and industries, such as coordinating people, communicating complex information, solving problems, managing resources and working with data. The US Department of Labor's O*NET database organizes skills into groups like these and its Skills Search matches them to occupations. Identifying yours usually means translating past work out of your current industry's jargon and into the vocabulary of the field you want to enter.

You suspect your skills would translate, but every time you try to name them you fall back on phrases like 'cross-functional alignment.' They meant something to your last manager. To a recruiter in a new field, they don't say much.

How to do it
1

How do you figure out what your transferable skills are?

Look at your last two or three years of work. For each major project or responsibility, write down what you did in plain English, with no industry jargon, no company-specific terms, no acronyms. Not 'led the cross-functional alignment for the Q3 OKR.' Instead: 'organized people from four teams to agree on a shared goal and a way to measure progress.' The plain version is the transferable version. The jargon was hiding it.

2

What are examples of transferable skills?

Now look at your plain-English descriptions and find the patterns. You probably did variations of the same handful of things over and over — getting people aligned, communicating with executives, breaking complex problems into steps, making decisions under uncertainty, managing a process. These are competencies. They show up in many jobs, under different names. List five to seven that you do well. These are your transferable skills.

3

How do you describe your skills for a different industry?

Each industry has its own vocabulary for the same competencies. 'Project management' in your field might be 'program management' or 'product operations' in another. 'Account management' might be 'customer success.' Read job descriptions in the target field and notice what phrases they use for the things you actually do. Adopt their vocabulary on your resume and in interviews. This is not pretending — it is translation.

4

How do you prove a transferable skill to an employer?

A transferable skill claim is only useful if you can back it up. For each competency on your list, identify one to three concrete projects or moments that prove it. The time you got two warring departments to agree on a launch plan. The time you cut a process from three weeks to four days. These stories are what hiring managers actually evaluate. Skills without stories sound like resume filler. Stories with skills baked in sound credible.

5

Who can tell you if your skills will transfer?

Get coffee with one person who already does the work you want to do. Show them your translated resume. Ask them what lands and what does not. Their reactions will calibrate you faster than any amount of solo writing. They will catch jargon you missed. They will tell you what to cut. They will identify the competency you under-played. One such conversation can be worth many edits done alone.
