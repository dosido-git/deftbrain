How to Decide Between Multiple Side Projects (When You Cannot Do Them All)

To choose between side projects, favor the one that uses skills you already have, produces something beyond itself (skills, audience, contacts), and fits your real hours once you've doubled your time estimate. Commit to it for a fixed period, and write the others down as a queue for later.

  1. Favor the project that uses skills you already have.
  2. Favor projects that build something beyond themselves.
  3. Double your time estimate before deciding.
  4. Pick one and write the others on a queue.
  5. Commit for a set period, then review.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

When you have more side projects than time, comparing them on how exciting they feel today tends to mislead. More useful questions are how much each draws on skills you already have, what it produces beyond itself, and whether it fits your real weekly hours once the estimate is padded for the well-documented tendency to underestimate time. Choosing one for a fixed period, with the rest written down for later, keeps the others from competing for attention.

Three good ideas, ten hours a week. You know doing all three means doing all three poorly, and you keep starting all three anyway, because choosing one feels like losing the other two.

How to do it
1

How do you choose which side project to work on?

A side project that uses skills you already have produces results faster than one that requires you to learn something new. The fast-results project sustains motivation; the slow-results project loses momentum after week three. For each project, ask: how much of this draws on what I can already do, and how much requires me to acquire new capability before producing anything? The high-leverage project usually wins not because it is more ambitious, but because you will actually finish it.

2

Which side projects pay off beyond the project itself?

Some projects produce only the project. Others produce skills, network, audience, or material that enables future work. A blog post that argues your thinking on a topic produces the post — and also positions you, builds your audience, and clarifies your thinking for adjacent projects. A product launch produces the product — and also a network of users, a feedback loop, and credibility. Pick projects with positive externalities. The project that only produces itself has lower expected return than the one with a broader payoff surface.

3

How long will a side project actually take?

Side project time estimates are almost always optimistic, a pattern psychologists call the planning fallacy. The project you think will take four weeks will take eight. The one you think will take three months will take six. Multiply your gut estimate by 2x — sometimes 3x. Then ask: am I willing to commit that much real time, given everything else in my life? Many side projects stall not because the project was wrong but because the original time estimate was far too low. Estimate honestly and many of the close-call projects become obvious nos.

4

What should you do with the side projects you don't pick?

Once you have ranked the options, pick one. Put the other two on an explicit list of projects-not-being-pursued-now. Writing them down does two things: it acknowledges their value (they are not forgotten) and it removes them from the active mental load (they are not competing for attention). The list becomes a queue for after the current project finishes. Trying to run three at once is often a missing queue rather than a lack of focus.

5

How long should you commit to a side project before reassessing?

Commit to the chosen project for a fixed time — eight weeks, twelve weeks. At the review point, decide deliberately: continue, switch, or stop. The fixed window prevents you from feeling like you have signed up forever. It also prevents the other two ideas from constantly pulling at you, because you know they will get a turn at the review point. Open-ended commitments can feel permanent, which makes them easy to resist; a bounded one is easier to keep because it has an end.
