What “Per My Last Email” Means —and How to Respond

“Per my last email” means “as I said in my previous email.” It points back to information already shared. It can sound impatient, but the phrase alone doesn’t establish intent. Check the earlier message, identify what still needs answering, and respond to that.

  1. The phrase refers to information in an earlier email.
  2. Context matters; the wording alone doesn’t prove annoyance.
  3. Respond to the outstanding request or ask a specific question.
  4. When following up yourself, repeat the useful detail.
  5. Address recurring communication problems with concrete examples.

Updated November 6, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

A reply beginning “per my last email” can leave you wondering whether you missed something or someone is annoyed. You can address what needs doing without first settling what the sender meant.

How to do it
1

Is “per my last email” always passive-aggressive?

No. Someone may be pointing you toward an attachment, deadline, or explanation in a long thread. It can also express frustration about repeating information.

Compare: “Per my last email, the meeting is at 10. I’ve attached the agenda again.”

“Per my last email, I’m still waiting for the figures due Friday.”

The first supplies information again; the second emphasizes an unresolved request. Neither tells you everything about the sender’s feelings. The surrounding message and your working relationship provide more context.

2

How should you respond?

Start with what the email needs from you. You can address the request without settling whether the tone was intentional.

SituationPossible reply
You missed a detail“Thanks—I missed the revised date. I’ll use Friday.”
You need clarification“I found the earlier message. Does the Friday deadline apply to the draft or the final version?”
You already answered“I sent the figures Tuesday; I’ve attached them again here for convenience.”
You cannot meet the request“I can send the draft Friday. The final version will be ready Monday—would that work?”
It is only an informational reminderNo reply may be necessary unless acknowledgment was requested.

Use only the wording that matches what actually happened. A brief acknowledgment is enough when you missed something; you don’t need a lengthy apology.

3

What if the message adds dates or copies other people?

Specific dates and additional recipients can make a thread more formal. They may document a missed commitment, or simply give others the background they need.

Check whether the message introduces a deadline, changes responsibility, or asks for a decision. Respond with accurate facts and a clear next step. If the record is incorrect, correct it briefly:

“For clarity, we agreed Tuesday that the final version would be due Monday. I can send the current draft Friday.”

If a call would resolve confusion faster, you can suggest one and summarize any agreement afterward in writing.

4

What can you say instead of “per my last email”?

If you’re writing the follow-up, repeating the useful detail saves the reader from searching the thread.

What you needPossible wording
Refer to earlier information“The meeting is at 10; I’ve included the agenda below.”
Resend something“I’ve attached the file again for convenience.”
Get a decision“Could you confirm whether we should proceed by Thursday?”
Follow up on overdue work“The figures were due Friday. When can you send them?”

You can be firm by naming the request and timing.

Before: “Per my last email, I need your approval.”

After: “Could you approve the revised budget by Thursday? We need confirmation before placing the order.”

5

What if this keeps happening?

Repeated friction deserves attention, especially when requests remain unclear or the exchange affects your work. Focus on specific examples and their impact rather than trying to prove passive aggression.

You might say: “We’ve had a few exchanges where I couldn’t tell what still needed answering. Could we put the outstanding request and deadline in the latest message?”

For an isolated email, begin with the unanswered task. For a recurring problem, consider a separate conversation about how you work together.
