How to Name a Startup When All the Good Names Are Taken

When the obvious names are taken, look in places with more room: words from other languages, compounds of two short words, a real name plus a modifier, or an ordinary word used as a metaphor. Avoid forcing a taken name with odd spellings, and check trademarks before committing.

  1. Look at meaningful words from other languages.
  2. Combine two short, concrete words.
  3. Add a modifier to a word you want.
  4. Use an ordinary word as a metaphor.
  5. Don't respell a taken name to make it fit.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

When the obvious one-word names and domains are taken, startups usually find room in a few categories: meaningful words from other languages, compounds of two short words, a desired word plus a modifier, and ordinary words used as metaphors rather than descriptions. Domain availability is only one check; the US Patent and Trademark Office notes that a name can conflict with an existing trademark even when the two are not identical, if the goods or services are related.

Every name on your list is taken: the .com is parked, the social handle belongs to someone who posted twice years ago, and the trademark is registered across the country. You're starting to consider dropped vowels and extra Ys because that's what seems to be left. It isn't.

How to do it
1

Can you use a word from another language as a business name?

Latin, Greek, Italian, Japanese, Hindi, Yoruba — every language has words and roots that are far less crowded than English nouns. The constraint is meaning: pick a word whose meaning is relevant or evocative, not random, and check what it means and how it sounds in the languages your customers speak. Words from a language the founders know well are a natural place to start.

2

Are compound words good startup names?

Two short English words combined produce names that are still trademarkable, often have available domains, and read as natural. Snowflake. Dropbox. Mailchimp. YouTube. The pattern is two short, concrete words that suggest function or feeling. There are far more possible two-word combinations than single words, so the odds of finding an open one are better.

3

What if the name you want is already taken?

If the noun you want is taken — say, Anchor — try meaningful prefixes or suffixes that do not look like .ly or 2-letter hacks. Anchor Studios. North Anchor. Anchor Lab. The modifier specifies industry, position, or character without the manufactured-sound of made-up suffixes. The .com may be available with the modifier. The brand name in customer-facing contexts can drop the modifier later. Some established brands started this way.

4

Should a company name describe what the company does?

Stripe is not about stripes. Square is not about squares. Slack is not about slack. The name evokes a feeling or a metaphor that connects to the company's character without being literally descriptive. Real common English words are often available because they do not look like brand names — they look like ordinary words. The unobvious choice is the one nobody else has tried because it does not feel like a brand at first.

5

Should you change the spelling of a name that's taken?

If you find yourself adding a Y, dropping a vowel, or replacing a C with a K to make a taken name available, stop. Creative spellings cost you forever — they fail the radio test, fail the say-it-out-loud test, and require permanent customer education. The right move is not to torture a taken name into availability. It is to find a different name in one of the categories above. A name nobody has to spell for you is worth the extra search.
