What to do when you have 1000+ unread emails

With 1,000 or more unread emails, declare email bankruptcy: archive (don't delete) everything older than about 30 days, message the few people you know are waiting, triage the last month quickly, and set up filters so bulk mail doesn't pile up again. Archived mail stays searchable.

  1. Archive, don't delete, everything older than about 30 days.
  2. Message the few people you know were waiting.
  3. Triage the most recent month quickly.
  4. Filter newsletters and notifications automatically.
  5. Let go of the guilt; it's a common outcome.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Email bankruptcy is the practice of clearing an unmanageable inbox in one step instead of reading through the backlog. Typically it means archiving, not deleting, everything older than a set cutoff such as 30 days, so messages remain searchable; contacting the few people known to be waiting; triaging the most recent weeks; and setting up filters for newsletters and notifications so the backlog doesn't rebuild. Senders with real needs usually follow up.

The counter says 1,247. Some messages are from last quarter, some from people who've given up waiting, and a few are urgent things you missed. You can't tell which, and looking at the number makes you want to close the laptop.

How to do it
1

What is email bankruptcy and how do you do it?

If an email is more than thirty days old and you have not responded, you are unlikely to now. Archive everything older than thirty days. All of it. Do not read first. Just archive. The archived emails are not deleted — you can still find them by search if something specific comes up. But they are out of your active pile. This takes seconds and usually removes most of the unread count. Your inbox is now a manageable size.

2

What should you tell people after declaring email bankruptcy?

If you can think of two or three specific people who were waiting on a reply from you, send each one a quick note. 'Sorry for the silence — my inbox got away from me. Please resend if you need a response on anything.' This puts the ball back in their court. If they actually needed something, they will resend. If they did not, the issue is closed. Many people appreciate the honesty more than a much-delayed full reply.

3

How do you get through the most recent emails?

Now you have a few hundred emails from the last month. Sort by sender or subject. Use the same fast-triage protocol you would use on a daily basis. Identify what genuinely needs a reply now, what can be archived without reply, what can wait. With discipline, the last thirty days can often be processed in well under an hour. Do not get sucked into long threads. Reply briefly or defer.

4

How do you keep your inbox from getting out of control again?

Once you are out of the hole, take fifteen minutes to set up rules that auto-archive newsletters, system notifications, and low-priority sender categories. The reason your inbox got to 1,200 is partly that low-value email was mixing with high-value email. Separating them programmatically prevents the same accumulation from happening again. Without a change to the incoming flow, the backlog tends to rebuild.

5

Is it okay to ignore old unread emails?

Inbox bankruptcy is not a moral failure. It is a normal consequence of email volume exceeding processing capacity. People you respect have done it. Some of them do it once a year. The shame people attach to it is disproportionate to the actual cost — most of the world keeps running. Once you have declared bankruptcy, you are done. There's little to gain from revisiting whether it was the right move.
