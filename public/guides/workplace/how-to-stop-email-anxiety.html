How to stop email anxiety

To ease email anxiety, turn off email notifications, check email in a few set windows, ask what would really happen if a message waited until tomorrow, and find out what response time is actually expected. If the dread persists, the cause may be workload or expectations rather than the inbox.

  1. Turn off email notifications.
  2. Check email in a few set windows.
  3. Ask what happens if it waits until tomorrow.
  4. Find out the real expected response time.
  5. If it persists, look at workload, not the inbox.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Email anxiety, the persistent unease about unread or unanswered messages, tends to come from constant notifications, never knowing what has arrived, and assumptions about how fast replies must be. Common ways to reduce it include turning off notifications, checking email at set times, clarifying expected response times with a manager, and noticing that genuinely urgent matters often arrive by phone or in person. When it persists despite those changes, workload is often the underlying issue.

It isn't any one email. It's the number on the icon all day, the sense someone is waiting, the 2 a.m. thought about a message you saw at 6 p.m. Most email advice is about volume, and it doesn't touch the feeling.

How to do it
1

Should you turn off email notifications?

A major source of low-grade email anxiety is the constant stream of notifications. Each ping pulls your attention and reminds you of what's waiting. Turn off email notifications on your phone and on your computer. Open email when you choose to, on your schedule, not when it pings. For many people, this change alone makes a noticeable difference. The cost — sometimes seeing an urgent email an hour later — is much smaller than the cost of being on alert all day.

2

How often should you check email to reduce anxiety?

Decide when in your day you will check email — say, three windows of thirty minutes. During those windows, process. Outside of them, do not look. The anxiety lives in the gap between checking — the wondering if something has come in. Closing that gap by structuring it makes the anxiety subside. The first few days are hard because the habit of checking is reflexive. For many people, it eases once the new habit settles in.

3

How do you stop worrying about unread emails?

When you feel anxious about unread email, ask: what is the worst thing that could be in there, and how bad would it actually be if I read it tomorrow instead of right now? In most cases, the answer is 'nothing terrible.' Genuine emergencies usually arrive by phone, text or in person. Email anxiety is mostly anxiety about feeling behind, not about actual catastrophe. Naming this gently to yourself helps.

4

How quickly do you need to reply to work emails?

Some email anxiety comes from the assumption that responses must be fast. They do not. Many workplaces are comfortable with a reply within a day or so for routine email. If your context expects faster, examine whether that expectation is reasonable — sometimes it is, but often it has been internalized without anyone actually demanding it. Ask explicitly: what is your normal expected response time? The answer is often longer than people assume.

5

What if email anxiety doesn't go away?

Sometimes email anxiety is not really about email — it is about a job that is overwhelming, a boss whose expectations are unreasonable, or a workload that exceeds the time available. If you have implemented the structural fixes and the anxiety remains, the email is the symptom, not the cause. The conversation to have is about workload and expectations, not about your inbox. Email anxiety that has structural roots will not go away with better email practices alone.
