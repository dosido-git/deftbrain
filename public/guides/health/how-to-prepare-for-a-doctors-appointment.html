How to Prepare for a Doctor's Appointment (Without Forgetting What You Came For)

To prepare for a doctor's appointment, write down your top concerns with the most important first, keep a short log of each symptom, bring every medicine and supplement you take, write your questions down, and decide what a successful visit looks like. Bringing someone to take notes helps.

  1. List your concerns, most important first.
  2. Log each symptom: when, where, how often, how long.
  3. Bring every medicine, vitamin and supplement.
  4. Write your questions down.
  5. Decide what a successful visit looks like.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Preparing for a doctor's appointment means arriving with three things written down: your concerns in order of importance, the details of each symptom, and a complete list of medicines, vitamins and supplements with doses. The US National Institute on Aging also suggests bringing insurance cards, other doctors' contact details, records the doctor doesn't have, and a companion who can take notes. A visit is short, so the most important concern goes first.

You're in the waiting room rehearsing what you came to say. The list was clear last night; now the three things that brought you here have blurred into one worry. A little work the night before survives the moment you walk in.

How to do it
1

What should you write down before a doctor's appointment?

Appointments are short, and most of the time goes to whatever you mention first. Pick the top three things you want addressed, write them on a single page or phone note, and start with the one that worries you most. The headache that has woken you four times this month doesn't belong in slot three.

2

How do you keep track of symptoms before a visit?

'I've been getting headaches' is a sentence the doctor has heard a thousand times. 'I've had headaches on the right side, behind the eye, lasting two to four hours, four times in the past three weeks, usually starting around 4 PM' is a different conversation. Write down when it started, where it is, how often, how long it lasts, what it feels like, and what makes it better or worse. Specific symptoms get specific responses.

3

What medications should you bring to a doctor's appointment?

Bring everything: prescriptions, over-the-counter, vitamins, supplements, herbal stuff, the magnesium your friend told you about. Photos of the bottles work fine. Doctors aren't trying to judge you for what you take — they're trying to spot interactions, and they can't if they don't know. The supplement that 'doesn't really count' is exactly the supplement that matters.

4

What questions should you ask at a doctor's appointment?

'What is this?' isn't a useful question — it's a topic. Useful questions sound like: 'What's the most likely cause?' 'What else could it be?' 'What should I watch for that means I should call back sooner?' 'What's the timeline you'd expect this to follow?' Write them down. You won't remember in the room. The doctor isn't going to volunteer the answer to a question you didn't ask.

5

How do you know if a doctor's visit went well?

If you don't define what success looks like, the visit ends whenever the doctor decides it does — and you'll get in the car not sure whether anything happened. Was the goal a referral? A prescription? Just a conversation about whether something is worth worrying about? Name it before you walk in. If the visit ends and you didn't get the thing you came for, that's the moment to ask one more question — not the moment to stand up and leave.
