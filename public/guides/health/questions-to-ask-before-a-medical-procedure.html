Questions to Ask Before a Medical Procedure (Before You Say Yes)

Before agreeing to a procedure, ask what it involves and what it is meant to change, why it is recommended for you now and what happens if you wait, what the alternatives are (including doing nothing for now), what the common and serious risks are for someone like you and how often this team does it, what recovery looks like, and what it will cost you after insurance.

  1. What is it, and what is it supposed to change for me?
  2. Why now, and what happens if I wait or do nothing?
  3. What are the alternatives, and why this one?
  4. What are the risks for me, and how often does this team do it?
  5. What does recovery look like, and what will it cost me?

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

A doctor recommended a procedure. It might be a scope, an injection, a biopsy, a joint replacement or something you had never heard of until twenty minutes ago. The appointment was short, the doctor sounded sure, and you said you would think about it. Now you are at home, and the questions you didn't think to ask are arriving one at a time.

Asking questions before you agree is not second-guessing your doctor. Informed consent exists so that you understand what you are agreeing to, and good clinicians expect questions. The ones below are the questions that most often change what people decide, or at least how they feel about deciding.

How to do it
1

What is it, and what is it supposed to change?

Ask for the plain-language version: what happens, how long it takes, whether you will be awake, and what the doctor expects to be different afterwards. 'What will this tell us, or fix, that we don't know or have now?' is the core question. For a diagnostic procedure, ask what happens next for each likely result; for a treatment, ask how much improvement is realistic and how soon. If the honest answer is 'it may not change much', that is worth knowing before you agree.

2

Why now, and what happens if I wait?

Some procedures are urgent; many are not, and the difference matters. Ask whether waiting a few weeks or months would change your options or your outcome, and what you would watch for in the meantime. 'What would make you say we should do this sooner?' and 'What would make you say we don't need it?' turn a yes-or-no into a picture of the decision. Watchful waiting is a real option for some conditions, not a failure to act.

3

What are the alternatives, including doing nothing for now?

Ask what else could be done: medication, physical therapy, a less invasive version, a different specialist's approach, or monitoring. Then ask why this option is being recommended over those for you specifically. If the alternatives were never mentioned, asking for them is reasonable, and so is a second opinion before a major or elective procedure. A clear answer to 'why this one' is one of the best signs the recommendation fits your situation.

4

What are the risks for me, and how often does this team do it?

General risk lists are long and hard to use. Ask which complications are common, which are rare but serious, and whether anything about you (age, other conditions, medications, previous surgery) changes those odds; ask about anesthesia separately if you will have it, and about the risks of not having the procedure, so the comparison is two-sided. For many procedures, teams that do them often tend to have better results, so 'How many of these do you do in a typical year?' and 'Where will it be done, and why there?' are normal questions. A doctor who answers them plainly is giving you useful information, not taking offence.

5

What does recovery look like, and what will it cost me?

Ask how long until you can drive, work, lift and exercise, whether you will need someone with you, and what warning signs mean you should call. Then ask about money before the date is set: whether it needs prior authorization from your insurer, whether every provider involved is in network (including anesthesia and pathology), and what your out-of-pocket estimate is. In the US, you can ask the provider for a written estimate.
