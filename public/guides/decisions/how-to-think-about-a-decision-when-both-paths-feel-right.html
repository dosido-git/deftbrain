How to think about a decision when both paths feel right

When both paths feel right, stop searching for the correct answer, since there may not be one. Name the value each path serves, notice which value you've neglected lately, use a coin flip to surface your preference, and then commit fully to the path you choose.

  1. Accept there may be no single right answer.
  2. Name the value each path honors.
  3. Notice which value you've neglected lately.
  4. Use a coin flip to reveal your preference.
  5. Commit fully once you choose.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

When two paths both feel right, the tie usually reflects two real values pulling against each other, such as stability and adventure or family and career, rather than a missing fact. More analysis rarely breaks it. Naming the value behind each option, asking which one has been neglected recently, and committing fully once you choose are more useful. Psychologist Daniel Gilbert's research suggests people tend to grow more satisfied with choices they treat as final.

If one option were obviously wrong, you'd have decided last week. Both are genuinely good, in ways that can't be measured against each other, and you keep waiting for one to feel clearly correct.

How to do it
1

What if there is no right answer to a decision?

When two paths are genuinely tied, there is no right answer. Continuing to search for one is the source of the paralysis. Accept, all the way, that whichever you choose will be a defensible choice that ignores some real value, and that is fine. Accepting that often relieves a good deal of the pressure. You are not failing to find the answer — there is no answer to find.

2

What values is each option serving?

Each option is good because it serves a specific value. One job is good because it is exciting; the other is good because it is stable. One city is good because it is closer to family; the other is good because it offers more career growth. Name the values explicitly. Now you are not choosing between options — you are choosing between values. That is harder, but at least it is the actual question.

3

Which value have you been neglecting?

Looking back over the last few years, you can often see which value you have been over-prioritizing. They worked too much. They moved for ambition again. They stayed put for safety again. The path that serves the under-weighted value is often the right one — not because it is objectively better, but because life is a moving average and you are due for a correction. The version of you who is reading this in two years is the one you should be choosing for.

4

Can a coin flip help you decide?

Flip a coin and assign one outcome to each path. Look at the result. Notice your reaction. Relief means you wanted that outcome. Disappointment means you wanted the other one. Do not actually let the coin decide — use it to surface the preference your brain has been refusing to articulate. The coin is a flashlight, not a verdict. The verdict is whatever feeling came up when the coin landed.

5

How do you make your choice the right one?

Some of what makes a decision feel right in retrospect is not the decision itself but the energy you put into making it work. Once you choose, throw your weight behind it. Stop comparing it to the path not taken. Build the new life, take the new job, commit to the relationship — fully. The path that gets your full effort tends to be the right one in the rear view, regardless of which path it was. Tied decisions are made right by what you do after.

Sources

  • Gilbert and Ebert (2002), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology: Decisions and Revisions: The Affective Forecasting of Changeable Outcomes (PDF, Harvard)
