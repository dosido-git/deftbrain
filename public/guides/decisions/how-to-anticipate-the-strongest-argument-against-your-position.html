How to Anticipate the Strongest Argument Against Your Position

To anticipate the strongest argument against your position, read the most serious person who disagrees in their own words, state why a reasonable person holds their view, find the assumption your view depends on, ask which facts would weaken it, and then try to answer that best version.

  1. Read the strongest opponent directly, not a summary by allies.
  2. Explain why a reasonable person would hold their view.
  3. Find the assumption your position rests on.
  4. Ask which facts, if true, would weaken your case.
  5. Try to answer the strongest version, not a weak one.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Anticipating the strongest argument against your position, often called steelmanning, means building the best version of the opposing case before anyone presents it to you. It works from the opponent's own best writing, the values that make their view appealing, the assumption your view depends on, and the evidence that cuts against you. The aim is not to defeat a weak version but to find out whether your position survives a strong one.

You've thought about your position, maybe deeply. Then someone makes a point you genuinely hadn't considered, and you realize you'd been arguing with a cartoon of the other side. The habit that prevents that moment can be practiced.

How to do it
1

Where do you find the strongest version of the opposing argument?

Don't predict the opposing case from scratch — find someone who's already made it and read them carefully. Pick the most intellectually serious person on the other side, not the loudest. Read them in their own words, not paraphrased by people who agree with you. Steelmanning often fails when the opposing case arrives secondhand, filtered through allies who flattened the strongest parts. Go to the source.

2

Why would a reasonable person hold the opposite view?

A strong version of an opposing argument shows what's appealing about the position to a reasonable person — not what's wrong with it. Try to articulate why a thoughtful person would hold this view, in terms that connect to values you also hold. If you can't find the appeal, you haven't yet understood the position. Most positions held by smart people have a real underlying logic; finding it is the work.

3

What assumption does your position depend on?

Your position rests on some assumption — about how people behave, what's likely to happen, what matters most. Find it. Then ask: what argument would attack this assumption directly? That's usually the strongest counter. If your position assumes markets correct themselves, the strongest opposition will challenge that; if it assumes a particular value should win, the strongest opposition will surface a competing value. The assumption is the load-bearing wall. Test it.

4

What evidence would weaken your position?

Most positions rely on some claim about facts: about trends, outcomes, evidence. The strongest opposition will often challenge the empirical case directly — by citing evidence that points the other way, or by reinterpreting the same evidence. Anticipate this. What facts, if true, would weaken your position? Are any of them actually true? An honest audit of the empirical case is often where weak positions get caught and strong ones get reinforced.

5

How do you test a steelman of the other side?

Once you've built the strongest version of the opposing case, try to defeat it. Not by repeating your original arguments — by engaging with the steelman directly. If you can defeat it, your position is in better shape than it was. If you can't, you've discovered something important about the limits of your view. Either outcome is valuable. The version of you that's done this work is the one whose conclusions actually hold up.
