Is This Health Claim Actually Backed by Evidence?

A health claim is well backed when several independent studies, ideally randomized trials, find the same result, the effect is big enough to matter in absolute terms, and the research isn't funded only by people who profit from it. One small or observational study is a starting point, not proof.

  1. Look for replication by independent groups.
  2. Check whether the study shows cause or only association.
  3. Ask for the absolute size of the effect.
  4. Note who funded the research.
  5. Treat one small study as a hypothesis.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Whether a health claim is backed by evidence depends on how many independent studies support it, what kind of studies they are, and how big the effect is. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health notes that the strongest evidence comes from several studies by different investigators. Randomized trials can show cause; observational studies show association. A Cochrane review found that drug studies sponsored by industry more often report favorable results than studies with other funding.

A podcast says cold plunges reduce inflammation. A bestseller says a supplement reverses aging. Each comes with confident phrasing and a study or two. Some of it is probably true, and you can't tell which without a weekend of reading. These checks speed that up.

How to do it
1

How many studies does it take to prove a health claim?

A claim with one supporting study is a hypothesis. A claim with five independent studies showing the same thing is evidence. Citation count is not the same — one study can be cited a thousand times and still not have been replicated. The question to ask is: how many independent research groups have tested this and found the same thing? If the answer is one or zero, downgrade your confidence regardless of how loud the claim is.

2

What's the difference between correlation and causation in health studies?

Many health studies are observational: they find that people who do X tend to have Y. They cannot show that X causes Y. A claim like 'eating walnuts reduces heart attacks' from observational data is consistent with reality and also consistent with 'people who eat walnuts also do other things that reduce heart attacks.' Real causal evidence comes from randomized trials. If the underlying study is observational and the claim is causal, the claim has been overstated.

3

What is the difference between relative and absolute risk?

A claim that 'X reduces risk of Y' could mean a 50% reduction or a 2% reduction. The first might change behavior; the second probably should not. Drill into the actual numbers — relative risk reduction, absolute risk reduction, hazard ratio. Coverage often quotes the more flattering relative figure. The absolute number can be small even when the study is real. Effect size is the part that's easiest to skip.

4

Does industry funding make a study unreliable?

A 2017 Cochrane review found that drug studies sponsored by manufacturers more often report results and conclusions favorable to the sponsor. This does not mean they are wrong — but it means they should be weighted carefully. The cold plunge industry, the supplement industry, and parts of the wellness influencer economy all have monetary reasons to amplify thin evidence. Independent funding plus consistent findings is the gold standard. Industry-funded plus a single study is the shakiest version.

5

Where can you check the evidence for a health claim?

Start with summaries from organizations that don't sell anything: the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (part of the NIH) for supplements and wellness practices, and Cochrane reviews, which pool the results of many trials, for treatments. When you read a claim, check four things: how many independent studies support it, whether they were randomized trials or observational, how big the effect is in absolute terms, and who paid for them. A claim that passes all four deserves more weight. One that rests on a single small, industry-funded or observational study is a reason to wait. For anything that affects your own treatment, talk it through with your doctor or pharmacist.

Sources
