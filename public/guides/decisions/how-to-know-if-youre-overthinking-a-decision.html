How to Know If You're Overthinking a Decision

You're probably overthinking a decision if you keep weighing the same factors, the stakes feel bigger than the realistic worst case, more research has stopped changing your view, you're rehearsing how to defend the choice, or part of you already knows the answer.

  1. You're cycling the same factors without new ones.
  2. The imagined stakes exceed the realistic worst case.
  3. Research has stopped changing your mind.
  4. You're planning your defense, not your choice.
  5. Part of you already knows the answer.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Overthinking a decision means continuing to deliberate after the thinking has stopped producing anything new. Common signs are repeating the same pros and cons, treating a small choice as if it were life-defining, researching past the point where new information changes your view, and worrying about how to justify the choice to others. Matching the amount of thought to the realistic stakes is usually the quickest way out.

It isn't an enormous decision: a coat, a class, an email, an application. But you've gone over it from every angle for days. Here's how to tell useful deliberation from a loop.

How to do it
1

How do you know if you're overthinking a decision?

Useful thinking surfaces new considerations; overthinking cycles the same ones. If you've been weighing the exact same five points for the third time, you're not learning anything new — you're just running the loop again. The test: write down what you're currently considering, compare it to what you considered an hour ago. If the lists are identical, the additional thinking isn't producing additional information. Stop the loop.

2

Why do small decisions feel so high-stakes?

Decisions that produce overthinking have often inflated in importance — the brain has decided that this small choice will determine your whole life. The actual stakes are often much smaller. Run the test: what is the worst-case outcome of getting this wrong? Articulate it specifically. Most worst cases are 'I'll be slightly inconvenienced' or 'I'll have to choose differently next time.' Once you've named the actual stakes, the volume of thinking should match them — and usually doesn't.

3

When should you stop researching and decide?

Reading more reviews. Asking more friends. Comparing more options. Some research is useful; past a certain point, more research isn't reducing your uncertainty — it's substituting for the decision. Notice when the research has stopped changing your view. If the last three things you read or heard didn't update your thinking, you have enough information. The further research is now a delay tactic. The next move is choosing, not learning.

4

Why do you worry about justifying your decision to others?

Some overthinking isn't about the decision; it's about how you'll explain it later. To your partner, your manager, your future self, an imagined critic. The energy goes into pre-justifying the choice, not making it. Notice when the thinking has shifted from 'which is right' to 'which can I defend.' These are different questions. The first one has answers; the second one rarely produces a clean one, because no choice is unambiguously defensible to every audience.

5

What if you already know the answer but don't like it?

Sometimes the overthinking is happening because the answer is already obvious to part of you and you don't want it to be the answer. The body has a different vote than the analytical brain — and when they disagree, overthinking is often the symptom. Notice the gut reaction. If you can identify it and name what makes it inconvenient, you're closer to the decision than the analysis is suggesting. The thinking was protecting you from acting on what you already knew.
