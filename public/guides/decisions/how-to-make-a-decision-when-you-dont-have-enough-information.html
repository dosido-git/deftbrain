How to Make a Decision When You Don't Have Enough Information

To decide without enough information, separate facts you could still get from uncertainty no one can resolve, think in ranges of outcomes rather than one prediction, run the cheapest experiment available, prefer the option whose worst case you can survive, and set a deadline.

  1. Separate facts you could get from uncertainty nobody can resolve.
  2. Think in ranges of outcomes, not one prediction.
  3. Run the cheapest experiment available first.
  4. Choose the option whose worst case you can survive.
  5. Set a deadline to decide regardless.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Deciding without enough information means separating two kinds of gap: facts you could still find out, and uncertainty no one can resolve yet, such as how a team or market will turn out. The first calls for a little more research; the second calls for thinking in ranges of outcomes, running small reversible experiments, choosing options whose worst case is survivable, and setting a deadline so that waiting does not quietly become the decision.

A job, a move, a partnership: the information you need is incomplete, and the people advising you don't have it either. The instinct is to keep researching until something resolves. For many real decisions, nothing will.

How to do it
1

What's the difference between missing facts and real uncertainty?

Not all 'I don't have enough information' is the same. Some decisions are missing facts you could actually get — by reading, asking, doing more research. Others are missing information that doesn't yet exist anywhere — outcomes, future market conditions, how a person will behave under future circumstances. The first kind needs investigation; the second kind needs decision-making under uncertainty. Decisions often get stuck when the second kind is treated as if it were the first and waiting for facts that won't ever arrive.

2

How do you think in probabilities when making a decision?

When you don't have enough information, you're not actually deciding about a single outcome — you're deciding about a range of possible outcomes weighted by likelihood. Don't pretend to know what'll happen; estimate a range. 'There's maybe a 40% chance the team is great, a 40% chance it's fine, a 20% chance it's bad.' Pick the option whose range you can live with, not the one that requires getting lucky. Decisions that work under range-thinking tend to be more robust than ones that bet on a specific outcome.

3

How can you test a decision before committing?

Sometimes you can buy information at much lower cost than committing to the full decision. A trial period, a single conversation, a small experiment, a six-week test. If a low-cost experiment can resolve a key uncertainty before you commit, it's usually worth running. Don't make the irreversible call when a reversible one would teach you the most important thing first. The framing isn't 'decide now' — it's 'what's the smallest move that gives me real information.'

4

How do you choose when you can't predict the outcome?

When the future is genuinely uncertain, the best decision isn't the one with the highest expected value — it's the one whose worst case is most survivable. If option A goes great, both A and B turn out well; if things go badly, A leaves you in a worse position than B. Take B. When you can't predict which world you'll end up in, favoring graceful failure over maximum upside protects you either way. To test the option you're leaning toward, write out the strongest honest case against it before you commit.

5

Should you set a deadline for a decision?

Lack of information is often weaponized into indefinite delay. Set a deadline by which you'll decide regardless of whether new information arrives. The deadline forces the decision; you can extend it once if necessary, but not three times. Waiting for 'one more piece of information' can go on indefinitely; the information keeps not being enough. The deadline is what closes the gap between gathering and choosing.
