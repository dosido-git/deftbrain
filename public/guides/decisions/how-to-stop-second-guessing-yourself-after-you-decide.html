How to stop second-guessing yourself after you decide

To stop second-guessing a decision, mark a specific moment when it becomes final, notice the what-if thought without arguing with it, stop researching the options you didn't pick, remind yourself why your choice was good enough, and turn your attention to what comes next.

  1. Mark the moment the decision becomes final.
  2. Notice the what-if thought; don't debate it.
  3. Stop researching after you've decided.
  4. Recall why the choice was good enough.
  5. Put the energy into what comes next.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Second-guessing after a decision means replaying a choice that can no longer be changed, usually by comparing it with an imagined better alternative. It rarely improves future decisions and tends to spoil the present one. Research by Daniel Gilbert and Jane Ebert found that people were more satisfied with choices they could not reverse than with ones they could, which suggests that treating a decision as closed helps it feel right.

The order is in. The offer is signed. Thirty seconds later your brain starts running counterfactuals, and by the time the food arrives you're grading it against an imaginary meal.

How to do it
1

How do you make a decision feel final?

Second-guessing often happens because the decision never officially closed. You picked, but you did not declare it picked. Build a closing ritual: when you tap "place order," that is the moment. When you sign the offer, that is the moment. After that moment, you are not allowed to revisit. This sounds silly until you try it. Naming the close turns the decision from a permanently open tab into a closed file. Your brain still pings you, but the pings have no purchase.

2

What do you do when you keep thinking 'what if'?

When the "what if I had picked the other one" thought shows up, do not argue with it. Do not list reasons your choice was correct. Do not pull up the other option to compare. Just notice the thought, label it ("oh, second-guessing"), and turn your attention to something else for thirty seconds. The thought is a habit, not a sign that the decision was wrong. Engaging with it strengthens the loop. Letting it pass without traction weakens it.

3

Should you keep researching after you decide?

Post-decision research is one of the quickest ways to feed second-guessing. Reading reviews of the place you ordered from after you ordered. Checking comparable salaries after you accepted the job. Looking at the shirt in better lighting after you bought it. None of this information is actionable, and when you're already doubting, the negative details tend to stand out most. Close the tabs. Stop researching things you cannot un-decide.

4

Is a good-enough decision really enough?

You did not need to make the optimal decision. You needed to make a good-enough decision, and you did. Restate, briefly, what made this option meet the bar: "it fit my budget, it was available tonight, it covers the constraint that mattered most." That is the relevant frame. The "best possible" frame is a trap, because no decision survives that test — there is always some imaginary better version. Good-enough is the frame that lets the decision actually be over.

5

How do you move on from a decision?

Every minute you spend grading the decision you already made is a minute you cannot spend on the next decision, the actual execution, or just enjoying the night. Once you notice the loop, the most useful question is "what is the next thing I want to do" — even if the answer is "nothing." Pulling forward is what closes the loop. Looking backward keeps it spinning. Sometimes what feels like a decision-making problem is really a post-decision rumination problem, and that one is often easier to fix.

Sources

  • Gilbert and Ebert (2002), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology: Decisions and Revisions: The Affective Forecasting of Changeable Outcomes (PDF, Harvard)
