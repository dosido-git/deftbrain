How to Politely Tell Someone They Are Wrong About a Fact

To politely correct someone's facts, first decide whether the error matters, correct them privately when you can, frame it as curiosity ('I'd heard differently, let's check'), and if you must correct in public, lead with the source rather than the person. Check that you're right first.

  1. Decide whether the error actually matters.
  2. Correct privately when you can.
  3. Frame it as curiosity, not correction.
  4. In public, lead with the source.
  5. Check your own facts first.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Politely telling someone they're wrong about a fact means getting the accurate information across without making them feel attacked. Useful moves are weighing whether the mistake matters, correcting in private where possible, presenting it as shared curiosity ('let's look it up'), pointing to a source rather than to the person, and confirming you're right before you speak. Done well, the conversation continues and the wrong fact quietly gets updated.

Your aunt says something wrong with great confidence at dinner. A colleague cites a 'fact' in a meeting that you know isn't true. You can correct publicly, privately, or let it go, and all three feel uncomfortable.

How to do it
1

Should you correct someone who is wrong about a fact?

Not every wrong fact needs a correction. Stakes vary. A wrong fact about a movie release date is low stakes; a wrong fact about a medical condition or a financial number can be high. The first question is whether the correction will matter to anyone. If your aunt says Christmas was originally a pagan holiday and the family agrees and moves on, the cost of correcting is higher than the benefit. If a colleague is about to make a decision based on a wrong statistic, the cost of not correcting is much higher. Calibrate.

2

Is it better to correct someone in private?

Public correction puts the other person on the defensive — even if your tone is gentle. Private correction lets them update without losing face. After the meeting, the dinner, the group chat, send a quiet 'hey, I went and looked it up because I was curious — turns out it is actually X, thought you would want to know.' Private corrections are often accepted gracefully. Public ones can produce defensiveness however kind the framing.

3

How do you correct someone without sounding rude?

'I thought I had heard differently — let me look it up' is curiosity. 'You are wrong about that' is correction. The first invites both of you to investigate together; the second positions you as the corrector and them as the corrected. Even if you already know the answer, framing it as joint investigation produces less resistance. The fact still gets corrected; nobody loses status.

4

How do you correct someone in a meeting?

Sometimes a wrong fact in a meeting or group conversation will mislead a decision and has to be corrected on the spot. In that case, lead with the source, not the correction: 'I want to double-check that — when I saw this last, the number was X, but let me confirm.' This positions you as questioning the data, not the person. By the time the correct number is established, the framing is collaborative, not confrontational.

5

How do you make sure you're right before correcting someone?

Check first. You might be the one who's wrong, and finding out before correcting is much better than after. Search the claim with a reputable source in mind: a government agency, a university, an established reference site or a professional fact-checker. Note where you found the answer so you can share it ('I looked it up, and here's what I found'). A correction with a source lands better than one based on a confident memory. And if credible sources disagree, the question may be genuinely contested, in which case a correction may not be appropriate at all.
