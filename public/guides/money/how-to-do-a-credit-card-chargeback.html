How to Do a Credit Card Chargeback

To do a credit card chargeback, first ask the merchant in writing for a refund, gather receipts and correspondence, then file a dispute with your card issuer, ideally online. Billing errors must be disputed in writing within 60 days of the statement. Answer any issuer follow-up promptly.

  1. Ask the merchant for a refund in writing first.
  2. Gather receipts, photos, tracking and messages.
  3. File through your issuer's portal and pick the closest reason.
  4. Billing errors: written dispute within 60 days of the statement.
  5. Answer the issuer's follow-up questions before its deadline.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

A chargeback is a reversal of a card payment that your card issuer makes after you dispute it. For credit cards, the Fair Credit Billing Act lets you dispute billing errors, such as wrong amounts or goods not delivered as agreed, in writing within 60 days of the statement showing the charge. The CFPB notes that for problems with the quality of goods or services, you generally must first try to resolve it with the merchant. Debit card protections are weaker and work differently.

The merchant won't refund you. You've called three times, and promised callbacks never came. The chargeback is the formal lever that doesn't depend on the merchant cooperating, but it has rules and deadlines, and it works best when you've done the groundwork first.

How to do it
1

Do you have to contact the merchant before a chargeback?

For disputes about the quality of goods or services, you generally have to make a good-faith attempt to resolve it with the merchant first, and your issuer will likely ask. Send one clear written request: what you bought, when, the problem, what you want (refund, replacement or fix), and a reasonable deadline such as 10 to 14 days. Email or the merchant's portal gives you a paper trail. If they refuse or ignore you, that record becomes evidence.

2

What evidence do you need for a chargeback?

Gather the receipt or order confirmation, the charge as it appears on your statement, photos of the defect or proof of non-delivery, copies of all communication with the merchant, and any return tracking numbers. Issuers decide disputes largely on documentation, so a complete, organized package helps more than a long explanation.

3

How do you file a chargeback with your card issuer?

Most issuers have a dispute option in their app or website, which creates a record. You can also call, but follow up in writing: the CFPB says a written billing-error notice is what protects your rights under federal law. You'll usually be asked to choose a reason, such as defective merchandise, services not received or an unrecognized charge. Pick the most accurate one; a mismatched reason can slow or sink the dispute.

4

How long do you have to file a chargeback?

For billing errors on a credit card, federal law requires a written dispute within 60 days after the statement containing the charge was sent. Card networks and issuers may allow longer for some dispute types, but don't count on it, and check your issuer's terms. The clock doesn't stop while you argue with the merchant, so start the merchant request early enough that you're still inside the window if it fails.

5

What happens if the merchant fights the chargeback?

After you file, the merchant can respond with its own evidence that the charge was valid. If your issuer asks you for more information, respond by the deadline it gives you; silence can be treated as dropping the dispute. Address each of the merchant's points with documentation. If the issuer decides against you, it must explain why in writing, and you can ask for the documents it relied on.
