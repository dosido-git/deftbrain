How to Spot a Bad Deal That Looks Good (Before You Click Buy)

To spot a bad deal that looks good, search the brand outside the marketplace, read the low reviews first, look for reviews bunched into a few weeks, compare the photos for inconsistencies, and read the return policy for overseas returns or restocking fees before you buy.

  1. Search the brand outside the marketplace.
  2. Read the low reviews before the five-stars.
  3. Watch for reviews bunched into a few weeks.
  4. Look for photos that don't match each other.
  5. Read the return policy before you buy.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

A bad deal that looks good is usually a low-priced product with thin brand history, inflated reviews and a return policy that makes returns costly. The FTC advises reading reviews from several sources, watching for bursts of reviews in a short period, and checking reviewers' histories. Since October 2024, an FTC rule has banned fake reviews and paying for reviews that express a particular sentiment, but such reviews still appear. Reading the return terms before buying is often the most telling check.

It looks like a steal: half the price of the brand-name version, mostly four- and five-star reviews, polished photos. Something feels slightly off, though: an unfamiliar brand, reviews that are all oddly enthusiastic. Usually it isn't an outright scam; it's a product that works for two months, with a return process designed to discourage returns.

How to do it
1

How do you check if a brand is legitimate?

Search the brand name on its own. A real brand has a website, a history, a presence outside the marketplace where you found it. A shell brand has nothing — maybe a thrown-together site, maybe nothing at all. If the brand name is a string of capital letters that sounds like nonsense (PRZIBOLT, KAMOJUKI), and there's no information about it beyond the product listing, you're dealing with a generic factory product with a fake brand wrapper.

2

Why should you read the worst reviews first?

Glowing reviews are the easiest to fake or incentivize. Low reviews more often describe real problems. Read them first and look for patterns: several reviewers describing the same specific failure, like a battery dying after two months or fabric tearing on the first wash. One complaint can be a fluke. The same flaw from several unrelated reviewers is a fact about the product.

3

What does it mean when lots of reviews appear at once?

Reviews for an established product usually build up gradually. A product with hundreds of reviews clustered in a few weeks, often right after launch, and very few since may have had an early push of incentivized reviews. The FTC lists a burst of reviews in a short period as a warning sign. Sort by date and look at the pattern; it doesn't prove anything on its own, but it's worth weighing.

4

How can product photos reveal a bad deal?

Look for inconsistencies. Listings for low-quality products sometimes mix studio shots, stock images and computer-generated or heavily edited 'lifestyle' scenes. If the product looks different from one photo to the next, or customer photos in the reviews don't match the listing, the item you receive may not be the one pictured.

5

What should you check before buying a cheap product online?

Bad deals often hide in the return policy. 'Free returns' might mean you pay shipping. 'Money-back guarantee' might require returning to an address overseas at your cost. 'Lifetime warranty' might be void the moment you've used the product normally. A legitimate seller has a clean, simple return policy. A predatory seller buries hostile terms in legalese. If the return policy is harder to find or read than the product description, treat it as a flag — not because every long policy is bad, but because hostile terms hide in length.
