What to Do With a Bill You Can't Afford (Before It Becomes a Bigger Problem)

If you can't afford a bill, contact the company before the due date and ask about hardship programs, extensions or payment plans. If you can't pay everything, protect housing, utilities and food first. Avoid high-cost borrowing, and consider a nonprofit credit counselor if unaffordable bills are becoming a pattern.

  1. Call before the due date and explain.
  2. Ask specifically about hardship programs and payment plans.
  3. If you must choose, protect housing, utilities and food first.
  4. Be cautious about covering bills with high-cost credit.
  5. For a recurring problem, talk to a nonprofit credit counselor.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

When a bill is unaffordable, contacting the company early usually leaves the most options open: extensions, payment plans, hardship programs or reduced amounts. Ignoring it can lead to late fees, collections, lawsuits and credit damage, the CFPB warns. For medical bills, check for errors and ask about financial assistance, which nonprofit hospitals must offer. If unaffordable bills are becoming routine, a nonprofit credit counselor can review your whole budget and help you weigh options.

The bill has been on the counter for two weeks. Looking at it makes it real, and the reality is you can't pay it, at least not in full, not by the due date. Avoiding it keeps the dread at a distance, but it also narrows your options a little each week. A few small, early steps usually make the biggest difference.

How to do it
1

Should you call a company if you can't pay a bill?

Yes, ideally before the due date. Before you miss a payment, you're a customer with a question; afterward, the account may already be moving toward late fees or collections. Even if you can't pay in full, an early call can open conversations about extensions, partial payments and hardship plans. Keep it simple: 'I'm not going to be able to pay this bill in full by the due date. What options do you have for this situation?' Many companies have options they don't advertise.

2

What is a hardship program and how do you ask for one?

Many types of creditors have some flexibility for customers in temporary difficulty. Utilities and phone companies may offer payment arrangements or assistance programs; medical providers have financial assistance and payment plans; credit card issuers sometimes offer hardship programs that lower interest or payments. Ask directly: 'I'm experiencing a financial hardship. What programs do you have for this situation?' Get any agreement in writing.

3

Which bills should you pay first when you can't pay them all?

If you have to choose, many counselors suggest covering essentials first: housing, the utilities you need, food and transportation to work. Next come secured debts where you could lose property, such as a car loan, and insurance you need to keep. Unsecured debts like credit cards, medical bills and store accounts usually follow. Within each group, consider which bills have the most immediate consequences, such as a shutoff or eviction notice. Medical bills are often among the most negotiable, and the national credit bureaus have waited a year before reporting unpaid medical collections since 2023, which can give you time to sort them out.

4

Should you use a credit card or payday loan to pay a bill?

Be cautious. Credit card interest builds quickly, and payday and other short-term loans can be very expensive and hard to repay, sometimes leading to repeat borrowing. A payment plan with the original company, even with a late fee, is often cheaper than financing the bill with high-cost debt. A low- or no-interest option used carefully, with a realistic plan to repay, is different, but read the terms closely.

5

What should you do if you can never afford your bills?

If unaffordable bills are becoming a pattern rather than a one-off, it may be time for a broader look. Nonprofit credit counselors, many of them low-cost or free, can review your full budget, help prioritize debts, and explain options such as a debt management plan or, in some cases, bankruptcy. Check that a counselor is a nonprofit and is upfront about any fees. Reaching out early tends to leave more options open, and asking for help is a practical step, not a failure.
