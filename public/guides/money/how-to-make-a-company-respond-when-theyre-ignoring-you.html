How to Make a Company Respond When They Are Ignoring You

To make a company respond when it ignores you, switch to a dated written letter, file a complaint with the right regulator and tell the company, email an executive, post a factual public message if it is consumer-facing, and use a chargeback or small claims if money is owed.

  1. Send a written letter with a deadline, by trackable mail.
  2. File with the relevant regulator and mention the reference number.
  3. Email an executive with a short factual summary.
  4. Post publicly, calmly, if the company watches its feeds.
  5. Chargeback or small claims when money is owed.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

When a company ignores phone calls and chats, written and outside channels tend to get attention: a dated letter sent by trackable mail, a complaint to a regulator such as the CFPB, FCC or your state attorney general, an executive email, and, where money is owed, a card dispute or small claims court. The FTC recommends sending complaint letters by certified mail with a return receipt. The CFPB generally expects companies to respond to complaints it forwards within 15 days.

You've called, emailed, used the chat and followed up. The system seems built to wear you down, and it's working. Rather than giving up, change channels. Each step below raises the visibility of your complaint and reaches people with more authority.

How to do it
1

Does sending a letter by certified mail make a company respond?

It often helps. Phone calls and chats can vanish into queues; a letter sent by certified mail creates a delivery record and lands with a person. The FTC recommends certified mail with a return receipt for complaint letters. Send it to the corporate headquarters or the company's customer relations address, with a tight, factual account of what happened, your prior contacts, the resolution you want and a reasonable deadline.

2

Should you tell a company you filed a complaint with a regulator?

Yes, it can help. File with the right body for the industry: the CFPB for financial services, the FCC for phone, internet and cable, your state attorney general for most consumer purchases, the FTC for scams and deceptive practices, and the BBB if you like. Then send the company a short note: 'I have filed a complaint with [agency]; reference number [X].' Regulators like the CFPB forward complaints and expect responses, which gives the company a reason to resolve it.

3

How do you find an executive's email to complain?

Customer service is set up to handle volume; executive offices often have small teams for escalated cases. Look for someone senior, such as the CEO or head of customer experience. Company press pages, annual reports and professional profiles sometimes list contacts, and many companies use a standard format like firstname.lastname@company.com. Write a short, professional email, not angry, explaining the situation and what you want.

4

Does complaining on social media get results?

If the company is consumer-facing and active on social media, a measured factual post on Twitter/X tagging their handle often gets faster results than direct contact. Stick to facts. Avoid all-caps and threats. Companies that monitor social media have teams whose job is to defuse public complaints; those teams have authority. This works for retailers, airlines, banks, and telecoms. It does not work for niche B2B vendors who do not have a public profile.

5

What can you do if a company still won't respond?

If you paid by credit card, you can dispute the charge with your issuer; for billing errors, send a written dispute within 60 days of the statement showing the charge. If you paid another way and are owed money, small claims court is built for people without lawyers; dollar limits, fees and timelines vary by state. Either step turns a complaint someone can ignore into a process the company has to answer.
