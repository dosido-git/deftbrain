Why Does Everything Cost So Much More Than It Used To (It's Not Just Inflation)

Everything costs more than it used to mainly because of cumulative inflation, which the Consumer Price Index measures. Other forces add to how it feels: smaller packages at the same price, cheaper versions disappearing, prices that change with demand, and fees added at checkout instead of shown up front.

  1. Inflation compounds over years, as measured by the CPI.
  2. Shrinkflation: same price, less product.
  3. Basic versions disappear in favor of pricier ones.
  4. Some prices change with demand and timing.
  5. Fees added at checkout raise the real total.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Prices are higher than a few years ago mainly because of inflation, the general rise in prices that the US Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks through the Consumer Price Index, a measure of the average change in prices urban consumers pay for a basket of goods and services. Inflation compounds, so several years of moderate increases add up. Shrinking package sizes, which unit pricing helps reveal, and fees added at checkout can make your own costs feel higher than the headline numbers.

Three ordinary items, and somehow the receipt is $47. You remember when the same trip cost noticeably less. Inflation is real and does much of the work, but a few other changes add to the feeling. Naming them makes the receipt easier to read.

How to do it
1

How much of rising prices is inflation?

Most of the broad increase is inflation: the general rise in prices across the economy, driven by costs like wages, materials, energy and demand. The Bureau of Labor Statistics measures it with the Consumer Price Index. Inflation compounds: even moderate annual rates add up to a noticeably larger total over several years. Your own experience can differ from the average, because the CPI tracks a broad basket and your spending may lean toward categories that rose faster, such as groceries or rent.

2

What is shrinkflation?

Shrinkflation is when a product gets smaller while its price stays the same or rises: fewer ounces in the bag, fewer sheets on the roll, a shorter bar in a familiar wrapper. It works like a price increase but is easier to miss. Checking the unit price, the cost per ounce or per sheet, is the most reliable way to spot it; NIST promotes unit pricing as a consumer tool for exactly this reason.

3

Why can't you find cheaper versions of products anymore?

Sometimes the basic version is discontinued or harder to find, and the shelf fills with 'premium' variants at higher prices. If the entry-level product disappears, you're nudged into spending more even though no single price went up. Store brands, unit prices and shopping at a different retailer are the usual ways around it.

4

Do prices change based on demand?

In some industries, yes. Airlines, hotels, ride-hailing and event tickets commonly use dynamic pricing that moves with demand and timing, and some online retailers adjust prices frequently. That can mean paying more at busy times. Comparing across days or sellers and setting price alerts can help.

5

Why are there so many extra fees now?

Many purchases now carry separate fees: resort fees, service fees, delivery fees, processing fees. The headline price stays low and the real price appears at checkout. For live-event tickets and short-term lodging, an FTC rule in effect since May 2025 requires the total price, including mandatory fees, to be shown up front. For other purchases, compare final totals rather than advertised prices.
