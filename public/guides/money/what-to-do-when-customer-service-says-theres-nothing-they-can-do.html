What to Do When Customer Service Says There's Nothing They Can Do (Because That's Almost Never True)

When customer service says there's nothing they can do, it often means nothing that agent is authorized to do. Ask who in the company has that authority, ask about exception or hardship processes, write to the executive office, and use regulators, card disputes or small claims if internal routes fail.

  1. Ask who in the organization can approve it.
  2. Ask about exception or hardship processes.
  3. Write to the executive office with documentation.
  4. Use regulators, card disputes or small claims.
  5. Decide whether the issue is worth taking further.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

'There's nothing we can do' from a customer service agent usually describes the limits of that agent's authority, not of the company. Supervisors, specialist teams, executive offices and hardship programs often have more room. Outside the company, regulators such as the CFPB forward complaints and generally expect a response within 15 days, and the FTC recommends documenting every contact and stating clearly what resolution you want. Sometimes, though, the answer really is final.

You explained the situation. The agent was sympathetic and then said, 'Unfortunately, there's nothing we can do.' It sounded like a closed door. You suspect it isn't, but you're not sure what to say that doesn't either give up or turn hostile.

How to do it
1

What should you say when customer service says there's nothing they can do?

When the agent says 'there's nothing we can do,' a useful response is to gently surface the layer they're hiding. 'I appreciate that there's nothing you personally can do — could you tell me who in the organization has the authority for this kind of request?' This isn't combative; it's procedural. It accepts that the agent has limits while declining to accept that the company has none. The shift from 'we' to 'who can' often produces an immediate routing — to a supervisor, an escalation specialist, an executive office. The agent isn't being deceptive; they're just describing their own scope, and your question expands the conversation past it.

2

Do companies have exceptions to their policies?

Many do: hardship programs, supervisor discretion, customer-relations teams, retention offers. Policies can't anticipate every situation, so companies often keep some internal flexibility. Frontline agents may not have access to these processes or may not be trained to mention them. Ask directly: 'Are there any exception or hardship processes that apply to a situation like this?' If the agent doesn't know, ask whoever they transfer you to.

3

How do you contact a company's executive office?

When supervisors have said no, many companies have an executive or customer-relations team that handles escalated cases. Writing to a senior executive by name often reaches that team. Contact details are sometimes on the company's website, in press materials or in annual reports, and many companies use a standard email format such as firstname.lastname@company.com. Keep it calm, factual and documented. It doesn't always work, but it puts the case in front of people with more authority.

4

Where can you complain if a company won't help?

Regulators handle complaints in their industries: the CFPB for banks and financial products, the FCC for phone, internet and cable, the Department of Transportation for airlines, and your state attorney general for most consumer purchases. Many forward complaints to the company and expect a written response. Card disputes can address billing problems for card payments, and small claims court handles clear money owed without a lawyer; limits and fees vary by state. These take some effort, but they're designed for consumers to use.

5

When should you accept that a company won't help?

Sometimes 'there's nothing we can do' is accurate — for that specific company, for that specific case, within the framework they're willing to operate. The relevant question then isn't how to push harder; it's whether the issue is worth taking outside that framework. Some battles are worth the effort: meaningful financial harm, fraud, regulatory violations, contractual breaches you can document. Others aren't: small inconveniences, situations where the company's behavior was annoying but not actionable, cases where the time and stress of pursuing the issue exceeds the value of winning it. The skill isn't refusing to ever accept no; it's correctly distinguishing the no's that are worth challenging from the ones that are. Every hour spent fighting an unworthy battle is an hour you don't spend on the worthy ones, and the worthy ones — the substantive escalations to regulators or chargebacks or small claims — are where consumer leverage actually lives.
