How to Write a Formal Complaint Letter to a Company (That Doesn't Get Filed in the 'Crank' Pile)

A formal complaint letter should open with the outcome you want, then give a dated record: what happened, when, who you dealt with and reference numbers. Name the policy or law your request rests on, ask for a written reply by a set date, and keep the tone neutral.

  1. First sentence: the specific outcome you want — refund, repair or credit.
  2. A short chronology with dates, names and case numbers.
  3. The policy, contract term or law the request rests on.
  4. A deadline for a written reply, such as 30 days.
  5. Copies of receipts and earlier messages attached.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

A formal complaint letter is a written, dated request for a specific resolution, such as a refund, repair or credit, backed by a short factual record. The FTC recommends including your contact details, account and transaction numbers, product details, what you want, and copies (not originals) of receipts and other documents, and sending it by certified mail with a return receipt. A neutral tone and a clear reply deadline make the letter easier to act on and create a record for any later escalation.

You've decided to put it in writing, and now you're unsure how a formal complaint should sound. You don't want to seem unhinged, or so mild the company can ignore you. Letters that move companies read less like grievances and more like incident reports.

How to do it
1

What should the first sentence of a complaint letter say?

First sentence: what you want. 'I am writing to request a refund of $147 for service charges billed on March 14, 2026.' Not the story of the issue. Not your frustration. Not the back-and-forth with support. The specific, dated, dollar-amount-or-action outcome you're seeking. This single move changes how the letter is processed — instead of being read as a complaint that needs to be evaluated, it's read as a request that needs to be granted or denied. That makes it easier to route and answer.

2

What details should you include in a complaint letter?

Compare two sentences. 'Your customer service was completely unhelpful and the agent was rude.' vs. 'On March 14 at 2:47 PM ET, I spoke with agent Marcus (case #4471829). He stated the charge could not be reversed because the dispute window had expired, which contradicts your stated 60-day dispute policy.' The second sentence is harder to dismiss because there's nothing subjective to dismiss. Specific facts force specific responses; adjectives invite vague ones. Your goal in writing the letter is to make it expensive for the company to give you a non-answer.

3

Should you mention laws or policies in a complaint letter?

If your complaint rests on a policy, contract term or law, name it specifically. 'Per your published Terms of Service, section 4.2, refunds may be requested within 60 days.' 'Under the Fair Credit Billing Act, I am disputing this billing error.' 'Under [your state]'s consumer protection law...' This isn't a legal threat; it shows you've done your homework and that the reply needs to address that authority. Make sure what you cite actually applies; your state attorney general's consumer pages are a good place to check.

4

How long should you give a company to respond?

Open-ended complaint letters get filed indefinitely. Letters with deadlines get processed. 'I request a written response within 30 days of receipt.' This is reasonable, standard, and creates a record — if the company doesn't respond, the lapsed deadline is itself a fact you can cite in the next round (a regulatory filing, a chargeback, a small claims action). 30 days is the right ask for most consumer disputes; 14 for time-sensitive billing matters; 5 business days for active service interruptions. Whatever you ask for, ask for it specifically.

5

Why do formal complaint letters work?

A calm, specific, dated letter addressed to a senior person signals that the case may escalate, to a regulator, a card dispute or small claims court, and that changes the company's calculation about resolving it now. That's most of why formal letters work: they're a credible sign of next steps, not magic. They don't always succeed, but a well-structured letter gives the company a clear, easy way to say yes, and gives you a record if it doesn't.
