What to Say When You Cannot Afford Something Your Friends Are Doing

When you can't afford something your friends are doing, say so plainly and briefly ('I can't swing this one'), offer an alternative you can afford, don't over-apologize, keep showing up to cheaper plans, and if the gap is ongoing, talk with one close friend about lower-cost things to do together.

  1. Say it plainly; no invented excuse.
  2. Pair the no with an affordable alternative.
  3. Mention it once, then move on.
  4. Keep showing up to things you can afford, or host.
  5. If the gap is ongoing, talk to one close friend.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Declining a trip, dinner or event you can't afford usually goes best with a short, honest statement, an offer of something you can do instead, and no long explanation. Friends generally take a plain 'I can't swing this one' more easily than an elaborate excuse. The bigger risk to a friendship is gradually disappearing from plans; staying present through cheaper activities, or hosting, helps keep the connection while protecting your budget.

Your friends are planning a trip, and the number makes it an obvious no. You don't want to say it. Lie about a work conflict, name the money thing and watch people squirm, or put it on a card and stress for months. None of those go well. There's a more direct option that tends to work better.

How to do it
1

How do you tell friends you can't afford something?

'I can't swing it this time' is a complete sentence. It doesn't need a story about a furnace or a vet bill. Much of the awkwardness comes from invented reasons, which tend to sound slightly off and leave friends pretending to believe them. Saying it plainly is shorter and easier for everyone.

2

What can you suggest instead when you can't afford plans?

Closing with no is a wall. Closing with no, but is a door. I cannot do the trip, but I would love to do dinner the weekend you all get back / take you to coffee before you leave / be at the going-away thing. The alternative is what keeps you in the friendship instead of being absent during a thing you all will reference for years. Pick something you can afford. Make sure you actually do it.

3

Should you apologize for not being able to afford something?

Once is plenty, if at all. Repeated apologies can read as guilt and make friends uncomfortable, which is what you were trying to avoid. Say it once, warmly, and move on: 'I can't do that one, but I want to hear all about it.' Then change the subject. Keeping it brief signals this isn't a crisis.

4

How do you stay close to friends who spend more than you?

The friendship-killing pattern is not declining a trip. It is starting to opt out of dinners, drinks, and group hangs because everything feels expensive. Pick the things you can afford, show up to those, and host occasionally — a dinner at your place, a movie night, a hike. Hosting is the cheapest social act and the most generous. Friends with different budgets keep their friendships when the lower-budget person stays present, not when they go quiet.

5

What if you can never afford what your friends want to do?

If the whole group has settled into a spending level you can't match, it may help to talk privately with the friend you're closest to: 'The group's been doing a lot that's hard for me to afford. Would you be up for some lower-key things, just us, sometimes?' This isn't negotiating with the group; it builds a one-on-one track that doesn't depend on the budget. Many close friends are glad to.
