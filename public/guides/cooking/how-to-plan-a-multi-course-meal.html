How to Plan a Multi-Course Meal Without Everything Going Sideways

To plan a multi-course meal, choose courses that don't all need the oven at once, write the schedule backward from the time you sit down, do as much as possible the day before, have one course fully ready before guests arrive, and leave a short pause between courses.

  1. Mix equipment: one cold, one stovetop, one oven course.
  2. Schedule backward from sit-down time.
  3. Make sauces, stocks, dressings and desserts the day before.
  4. Have one course plated and waiting.
  5. Pause between courses before plating the next.

Updated October 9, 2026 · By the DeftBrain team

Planning a multi-course meal is mainly a logistics problem: choosing dishes that don't compete for the same oven or burners, building a timeline that works backward from the moment you sit down, moving as much prep as possible to the day before, and keeping one course completely ready as a buffer. Make-ahead components should be cooled and refrigerated within two hours, as the USDA advises for cooked food, and reheated until hot.

Three or four dishes, served in order, for people arriving in five hours. You've made each one before; you've never made them in sequence. That's where it falls apart, with plates still on the table while the salad wilts and you never sit down.

How to do it
1

How do you choose courses for a multi-course dinner?

A common multi-course problem is choosing dishes that all need the same equipment at the same time. Two oven dishes, three stovetop dishes and a salad don't fit through one oven and four burners. A cold starter, a stovetop main and an oven dessert do. Map the equipment before finalizing the menu, and if two dishes compete, swap one. A useful pattern is one cold course, one stovetop course, one oven course, and one served at room temperature.

2

How do you make a timeline for a dinner party?

Decide what time guests sit. Then write the schedule in reverse. Plate at that time. Sear the main ten minutes earlier. Pull the appetizer from the fridge fifteen minutes earlier. Mise en place by an hour earlier. Shopping done by morning. Working backward from the finish is a schedule that respects the constraint that matters — when food meets table — instead of just listing tasks in the order you thought of them.

3

What can you make the day before a dinner party?

Many components hold well overnight: sauces, stocks, braises, soups, vinaigrettes, and a lot of desserts. Almost anything that isn't searing, plating or a last-minute texture (crisp, fried, freshly dressed greens) can move to the day before. Cool cooked food promptly and refrigerate it within two hours; shallow containers cool faster. Reheat sauces, soups and braises until steaming hot. The aim is to start the day with the meal mostly cooked rather than starting from raw ingredients a few hours before dinner.

4

Should one course be ready before guests arrive?

Whether it is the appetizer, the salad, or the dessert — at least one course should be plated, garnished, and waiting in the fridge before anyone rings the bell. That course is your buffer. If everything else gets behind, you have one ready-to-serve component buying you time. It also gives you something to hand guests in the first ten minutes while you finish the rest.

5

How long should you wait between courses?

Do not try to plate the next course while you are clearing the last one. Sit down between courses for five minutes. Refill drinks. Let people talk. Then get up and plate. The pause is for two reasons: it gives you a beat to think about the next course's timing, and it makes the meal feel like a meal instead of a march. Restaurants pace courses on purpose. Your dinner should pace too.
