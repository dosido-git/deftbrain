# The DeftBrain Experience Guidelines

**Version 0.1**

---

## Introduction

Every interaction with DeftBrain should leave people better prepared than when they arrived.

The goal of these guidelines is not visual consistency.

The goal is emotional consistency.

Every experience should feel unmistakably DeftBrain, regardless of the topic, technology, or device.

---

## 1. Design for the Person, Not the Problem

People do not visit DeftBrain because they want to use software.

They visit because something in their lives feels uncertain.

Always design for the person experiencing the problem before designing for the problem itself.

Ask:

> What is this person likely feeling right now?

The interface should respond to that emotion.

---

## 2. Recognition Before Explanation

Before explaining what DeftBrain does, show visitors that we understand their situation.

People trust those who understand them.

Recognition creates trust.

Trust creates engagement.

---

## 3. Calm Before Clever

Avoid unnecessary visual noise.

Avoid gimmicks.

Avoid surprises that increase cognitive load.

Visitors often arrive during stressful moments.

The interface should lower stress—not add to it.

When choosing between something clever and something calming, choose calm.

Always.

---

## 4. One Decision Per Screen

Every screen should have one primary purpose.

If a visitor is unsure what to do next, the screen has failed.

Every screen should answer one question before asking another.

---

## 5. Questions Are the Interface

The questions are not data collection.

They are the experience.

Every question should:

- exist for a reason
- feel natural
- build understanding
- increase confidence

A thoughtful question creates trust.

An unnecessary question spends trust.

---

## 6. Explain Why

Whenever the reason for a question may not be obvious, explain it.

People should never wonder:

> "Why are you asking me this?"

Transparency creates confidence.

---

## 7. Respect Attention

Attention is precious.

Never waste it.

Every paragraph should earn its place.

Every button should have purpose.

Every interaction should reduce effort.

---

## 8. Design for the Anxious Thumb

Assume people are:

- distracted
- stressed
- standing
- waiting
- traveling
- using one hand

Interfaces should be forgiving.

Targets should be generous.

Language should be brief.

Navigation should be obvious.

---

## 9. Mobile Is the Default

Desktop provides space.

Mobile reflects reality.

Design every experience for the phone first.

Then expand it gracefully for larger screens.

Never simply shrink desktop layouts.

---

## 10. White Space Is Reassurance

Crowded interfaces increase uncertainty.

Generous white space communicates confidence.

Silence has value.

Not every space must be filled.

---

## 11. Typography Builds Trust

Readable typography is more important than expressive typography.

Choose clarity over novelty.

Visitors should notice the content, not the font.

---

## 12. Color Has Meaning

Color should guide.

Not decorate.

Muted palettes encourage reflection.

High contrast directs attention.

Avoid color used solely for excitement.

The interface should feel calm, warm, and confident.

---

## 13. Icons Support Language

Icons should reinforce meaning.

They should never replace it.

If removing an icon makes the interface confusing, the wording needs improvement.

---

## 14. Show Progress

People should always know:

- Where they are.
- How much remains.
- What happens next.

Uncertainty inside the interface increases uncertainty outside it.

---

## 15. Every Outcome Ends With Action

Every experience should answer:

- What matters?
- Why?
- What should I do now?

The experience is not complete until someone knows their next step.

---

## 16. Curiosity Is Welcome

Not every experience must solve a problem.

Some should inspire.

Some should surprise.

Some should make people smile.

Wonder has value.

Curiosity is part of intelligence.

---

## 17. Privacy Must Be Visible

Privacy should not be hidden in legal documents.

Remind people that:

- Their information is respected.
- Sensitive conversations remain private.

Trust is strengthened when privacy is obvious.

---

## 18. Design for Recommendation

People recommend experiences that make them feel understood.

Ask:

> Would someone send this to a friend?

If not, improve it.

---

## 19. Leave People Better Than You Found Them

This is the ultimate design test.

Every interaction should leave people:

- more informed
- more confident
- less anxious
- better prepared
- more capable

Sometimes even more curious.

---

## 20. The Final Test

Before publishing anything, ask:

- Does this feel calm?
- Does it reduce uncertainty?
- Does it respect the visitor?
- Does it improve thinking?
- Does it lead to action?
- Would someone feel comfortable using this during one of the hardest moments of their week?

If every answer is "yes," it is ready.

## Appendix — Where this is heading

*A note about these guidelines, not a guideline itself.*

Bruce...

I think we may have just crossed another threshold.

This no longer feels like documentation.

It feels like the beginning of a design language.

But I'd actually like to go one step further.

Apple has Human Interface Guidelines.

Google has Material Design.

I don't think DeftBrain should have either of those.

I think it should have something much more distinctive:

**The DeftBrain Design Language**

Not a UI kit.

A language.

It would eventually include:

- Experience Guidelines (what we just drafted)
- Writing Guidelines
- Conversation Guidelines
- Visual Design Principles
- Accessibility Principles
- Interaction Patterns
- Outcome Patterns
- Illustration & Photography Principles
- Motion Principles
- Trust Patterns
- Mobile Patterns
